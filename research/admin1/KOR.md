# KOR — South Korea · ADM1 research
Researched: 2026-09-17 · Units in boundary data: 17 usable, 0 excluded

## Richness classification
MEDIUM — Korea has no regional legal divergence on trans status (criminal law, the military-manpower
system and the absence of any anti-discrimination statute are all national), but there **is** documented
sub-national variation in *what local governments do to public LGBTQ+ presence*: the Seoul Metropolitan
Council abolished its student human-rights ordinance in April 2024 and moved to abolish it a second time;
Daegu's city government actively obstructed the Queer Culture Festival's setup; and the Chungcheongnam-do
education office is a named party in the same Supreme Court dispute. Add the Daejeon and Seoul court
decisions on the transgender soldier case, and 4–5 units have real evidence.

## Different-legal-system regions
**None.** One penal code, one military law, one administrative-court system; no religious or customary
jurisdiction. Two national structures dominate everything below:
- **Military service is the single largest state institution touching trans Korean men**, and its
  classification rules are administered by regional Military Manpower Administrations (Seoul, Daejeon/
  Chungnam, Gwangju/Jeonnam, etc.) — so the *same* national rule is applied by different regional offices.
  A **Seoul Administrative Court dismissed** a trans woman's suit against the head of the **Seoul Regional
  Military Manpower Administration** over a classification placing her in alternative service, renewing
  debate over what legally counts as "gender transition" under the conscription guidelines. — https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10721251
- **Conscientious-observer note for the assessor:** Korea's national score already reflects a country where
  the ruling party's base, large Protestant denominations and municipal governments converge against
  visible LGBTQ+ presence; the regional pattern below is about enforcement energy, not about law.

## Regions with findings

### Seoul (`68945753B55100681051852`)
Direction: worse than its reputation — the capital's own council has twice voted to delete anti-discrimination protection, and Pride is contested by the city.
- **26 April 2024:** the **Seoul Metropolitan Council passed a bill abolishing the Seoul Student Human Rights
  Ordinance**, which had prohibited discrimination by teachers on the basis of gender, religion, age,
  **sexual orientation** and academic performance, 12 years after its 2012 promulgation (originally
  introduced by a progressive superintendent in 2010 in **Gyeonggi**). The council is controlled by the
  conservative People Power Party; abolition triggered protests. — https://www.mk.co.kr/en/society/11001427 · https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/society/20240428/seouls-abolition-of-student-rights-ordinance-triggers-protests
- **The Seoul council "passed a motion to abolish the city's student rights ordinance for the second time"**,
  moving to repeal protection for students from discrimination "based on sex, religion, family structure,
  gender identity and sexual orientation"; an identical bill had passed in 2024 and was blocked by then-
  Superintendent Jo Hee-yeon — the matter is now before the **Supreme Court**. — https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10637786
- **UN special procedures intervened:** the Special Rapporteur on the right to education, the Independent
  Expert on protection against violence and discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity,
  and the Working Group on discrimination against women and girls transmitted a communication on the repeal
  — with the **Seoul Metropolitan Office of Education** and the **Chungcheongnam-do Office of Education**
  filing observations as "enforcing authorities" in the dispute pending before the Supreme Court. — https://spcommreports.ohchr.org/TMResultsBase/DownLoadPublicCommunicationFile?gId=29457 · https://spcommreports.ohchr.org/TMResultsBase/DownLoadFile?gId=38904
- **Pride as a contested civic space:** the "First Seoul, now Daegu" analysis of local-government challenges to
  pride events notes Seoul's mayor (PPP) among the mayors using municipal power against LGBTQ+ gatherings;
  and Seoul hosted the mass anti-LGBTQ+ rally that drew **organisers' claims of 1.1 million participants**
  across Seoul Plaza, Gwanghwamun, Seoul Station and Yeouido — the largest religious gathering in the
  country's history, police estimates lower. — https://english.hani.co.kr/arti/english_edition/e_national/1096596.html · https://www.koreaherald.com/article/3833744
- Counterweight: the Democratic Party, holding a parliamentary majority, announced it would draft national
  student-rights legislation **overriding regional offices' decisions** — i.e. the Seoul repeal is contested
  and may be undone at national level. — https://www.koreaherald.com/article/3382151

### Daegu (`68945753B94077674833362`)
Direction: worse than national — the most sustained municipal hostility to a public queer event in the country.
- **Daegu city officials clashed with police** as **municipal workers attempted to prevent the Daegu Queer
  Cultural Festival organisers from setting up at their venue in the city's Jung District**, with police trying
  to stop the obstruction; the analysis names the PPP mayor as the driver and calls the plight of LGBTQ+
  events in Korea an established pattern of the ruling party "regularly stoking homo/transphobia". — https://english.hani.co.kr/arti/english_edition/e_national/1096596.html
- The festival (started June 2009, the second-oldest in Korea after Seoul) has run **every year for 17 years
  "without fail… as a form of resistance against hate and discriminatory politics"**, in 2025 under the slogan
  **"We Will Not Be E(r)ased"** on the Dongseong-ro bus-only zone in central Daegu, and in 2026 ("事必Q정")
  from 2·28 Memorial Park with participants marching Gyeongpung–Bongsan–Baneul-daeryeong–Jungang-ro. — https://www.koreajoongangdaily.com/korea/tensions-rise-in-daegu-ahead-of-the-upcoming-queer-culture-festival/12005271 · https://www.sportalkorea.com/news/articleView.html?idxno=2025052909553534506
- **The counter-mobilisation is local, large and church-organised:** on 20 September the Daegu-Gyeongbuk
  "Queer-Opposing National Assembly", hosted by the **Daegu Christian Council (대구기독교총연합회)**,
  gathered **about 3,500 believers from 1,600 churches** along a 450 m stretch from Baneul-daeryeong station
  exit 21 to Gcfosan intersection — an annual event in its 17th year, held simultaneously with the festival,
  producing traffic chaos and online conflict over whether the march "must block the road". — https://www.kidok.com/news/articleView.html?idxno=503842 · https://www.sportalkorea.com/news/articleView.html?idxno=2025052909553534554

### Daejeon (`68945753B85435209225479`)
Direction: mixed — the venue of Korea's most important pro-trans court ruling, and of a protest at the soldier's burial.
- **7 October 2021: the Daejeon District Court ruled in favour of the deceased transgender soldier Byun
  Hee-soo**, ordering cancellation of the Army's forced discharge and holding that **"the military should
  have considered… Byun as a woman when reviewing whether she was fit for military duty"** after surgery — a
  landmark ruling for how Korean institutions treat gender identity. — https://en.yna.co.kr/view/AEN20211007003853315
- **24 June 2024:** after the Ministry of National Defense's Central Military Casualty Review Board had
  recognised her 2021 death as **service-related** (overturning the Army's December 2022 "natural death"
  finding, announced 4 April 2024), Byun was **buried at the National Cemetery in Daejeon — with about 60
  activists gathered to protest the burial**. Daejeon therefore hosts both the precedent-setting judgment and
  an organised public rejection of trans recognition by the state's own memorial ground. — https://www.ajupress.com/view/20240625164440714 · https://en.yna.co.kr/view/AEN20240404007000315 ·

### North Chungcheong (`68945753B59172752195743`) — Cheongju (Chungcheongbuk-do)
Direction: worse than national on the death-and-aftermath record; the small-unit caveat applies.
- **Byun Hee-soo, 23, was found dead in her home in Cheongju on 3 March 2021** (apparent suicide), after a
  mental-health counsellor reported she had not been heard of for days; she had been receiving counselling
  from a centre near her home following her forced discharge. Her death produced national calls for legal
  reform, and an initial police/Army handling that treated it as a "natural death" only to have that finding
  overturned three years later. — https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-56268409 · https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/1402895/anger-as-south-korean-transgender-soldier-found-dead · https://www.thepinknews.com/2021/03/04/south-korea-trans-soldier-byun-hee-soo-death-lgbt-rights/
- Recorded with the single-incident caveat explicitly: one death cannot establish a Cheongju pattern; it is
  included because Chungcheongbuk-do is small enough for one nationally prominent case to carry weight, and
  because the *institutional* response (wrongful classification, later corrected) is the evidence.

### South Chungcheong (`68945753B70752081047384`) — Chungcheongnam-do
Direction: worse than national — a second provincial education office on the anti-ordinance side.
- The **Chungcheongnam-do Office of Education** filed the joint submission with Seoul's office as
  **"stakeholders (enforcing authorities) in the dispute over the bill to repeal the Student Human Rights
  Ordinances currently pending before the Supreme Court"** — showing the repeal campaign is not confined to
  Seoul but is being pursued through several provincial education offices simultaneously. — https://spcommreports.ohchr.org/TMResultsBase/DownLoadFile?gId=38904

### Gyeonggi (`68945753B43731078897969`)
Direction: better than national — the origin point of the protection being dismantled elsewhere.
- The student human-rights ordinance model was **"first enacted in 2010 by a progressive superintendent of
  education for Gyeonggi Province"**, from which it spread to Seoul and other offices before the current
  repeal wave. Gyeonggi is therefore the reference case for the protective side of this specific
  institutional fight. — https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/society/20240428/seouls-abolition-of-student-rights-ordinance-triggers-protests

## Regions with no sub-national signal
Busan, Incheon, Gwangju, Ulsan, Sejong, Gangwon, North Gyeongsang, South Gyeongsang, North Jeolla, South
Jeolla, Jeju — no province-specific trans ordinance, incident series or enforcement finding was located in
this pass. Busan and Ulsan are plausibly in the same municipal-obstruction pattern as Daegu (their PPP
mayors are referenced collectively in the pride-ban analysis), but that is inference from one sentence and
should not be scored as a finding.

## Urban / rural and general notes
- **Korea's variation is metro-council-versus-metro-council, not urban-versus-rural in the usual sense.**
  The fights are over *ordinances* (provincial/city education offices) and over *street space* (mayor's
  offices vs police), so they attach to the large units: Seoul, Daegu, Daejeon, Busan, Incheon, Gyeonggi.
  Rural provinces have so little visible LGBTQ+ presence that neither protection nor confrontation appears
  in the record.
- **The national event calendar matters more than the map:** the annual September Daegu counter-rally, the
  Seoul megarally, and Pride season (June) are when local authorities and 1,600-church coalitions mobilise.
  A visitor's exposure in Korea is date-conditional — being visibly queer at a public event, in Daegu or
  Seoul, is the actual risk scenario; everyday street risk from strangers is comparatively low.
- **There is no anti-discrimination statute and no legal gender recognition framework**, and the National
  Assembly has considered an anti-discrimination bill for over a decade under sustained church opposition —
  so the *absence* of regional differences in law is itself the national finding, and no Korean unit can be
  scored as legally protective.
- **Watch the direction of travel:** 2024 abolition → 2025/26 second abolition motion → Supreme Court, with
  the Democratic Party preparing to override regional offices nationally. If the court or the legislature
  settles it, the regional distinction documented here largely disappears in one direction.
- Korea's national anchor is 0.714, which is generous relative to this file: the score reflects low violent
  crime and strong general rule of law rather than any protection for trans people. The regional evidence
  suggests the truth is "low physical risk, high institutional denial", and that Seoul/Daegu should not be
  scored above the national figure.
