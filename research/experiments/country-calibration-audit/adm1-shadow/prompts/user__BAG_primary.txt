### NATIONAL ASSESSMENT
The country criminalises no conduct on the basis of gender identity or gender expression. Same-sex acts have been legal since the Supreme Court's December 2007 judgment in Sunil Babu Pant v. the national government, which held that homosexuality is not 'unnatural'. The Criminal Codes Act 2017, which replaced the 1963 Muluki Ain, still lists 'unnatural sex' on its face and must be read in light of that ruling; the Muluki Ain criminalised 'unnatural sexual intercourse' with up to one year's imprisonment, and many LGBTQI+ people were arrested under it. The age of consent is 18 regardless of gender or sexual orientation. Legal gender recognition rests on the 2007 Supreme Court ruling recognising the right to self-determined name and gender markers, and a third-gender category has been available on official documents by self-declaration. Government officials limited changes to people identifying as 'third gender' while changes to 'male' or 'female' markers lagged, so the progressive reputation outruns policy. A human-rights organisation called in February 2024 on the government to create a clear, simple, rights-respecting procedure for transgender and third-gender people to obtain official documents, indicating no straightforward document-change process exists; a trans woman photographed that month held a 2023 citizenship certificate listing female gender. On 30 August 2024 the Supreme Court ruled that a trans law student must be legally recognised as a woman on all documents without medical verification, after she filed more than 50 lawsuits beginning in 2021. Before that ruling, changing markers to 'female' or 'male' had usually required surgery abroad plus in-country medical evaluations including intrusive post-operative genital exams. Local activists assessed in September 2020 that an extensive overhaul of the law and the language of the law is still needed to address inequalities faced by the trans and broader PoMSOGIESC community. On 28 June 2023 a Supreme Court justice issued an interim order directing the government to 'temporarily register' marriages of non-traditional couples and sexual minorities, and the full bench has yet to deliver a final verdict. In April 2024 the national identity and civil registration department instructed local authorities to enter same-sex marriages into a separate register. Temporary registration grants no equal rights: such couples cannot inherit property, receive tax subsidies, make spousal medical decisions or adopt children, and certificates label them 'groom and bride'.
