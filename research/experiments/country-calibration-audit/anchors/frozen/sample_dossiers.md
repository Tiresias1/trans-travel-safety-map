## DOSSIER 1 (country)

### ASSESSMENT
The country's 2016 Penal Code, Article 274, punishes consensual same-sex conduct with six months to three years' imprisonment, a fine of GNF 500,000 to 1,000,000, or both. The provision replaced Article 325 of the 1998 Penal Code, which criminalised the same conduct through indecency provisions and set a lower minimum fine. Where the conduct involves violence or attempted violence, the sentence rises to five to ten years, and acts with a person under 21 carry the maximum penalty. Enforcement reaches people who are not charged with conduct. Police arrest on the basis of clothing and appearance, and Article 274 functions as a standing basis for extortion, with release commonly secured by payment to officers. A 2024 Universal Periodic Review stakeholder submission reports LGBTQ people held for up to five days despite the 48-hour legal limit, most released only after paying bribes or fines. Transgender women are arrested and imprisoned on the stated pretext of protecting them from community violence, then held in male prisons where they often experience sexual violence. LGBTQ people in custody are frequently abused. In 2023 the national office for the protection of women, children and morals added a unit to investigate same-sex conduct. Security forces have arrested, harassed and used violence against LGBTQ people accused of disrupting social order. In 2016 and 2017 a local LGBTQ organization reported gay men jailed and fined for release. The country provides no legal gender recognition and no recognition of same-sex unions. No laws protect LGBTQ people from discrimination in health services, and LGBTQ people face discrimination when seeking healthcare, housing, education and employment. A January 12, 2024 law on HIV Prevention, Care, and Protection defines members of sexual minorities to include gays, lesbians, bisexuals, intersex and transgender people whether or not they self-identify. A 2009 ordinance on HIV/AIDS lists sexual minorities as a vulnerable and marginalized group. A 2024 UPR stakeholder report describes a hostile, sometimes lethal environment for LGBTQ people, with threats of physical violence and discrimination on orientation, gender-identity and expression grounds, and widespread stigmatisation that hinders reporting of violence. A survey spanning 2021 to 2023 found 87 percent of the population would dislike or strongly dislike having a gay neighbor. A 2023 human rights report found that LGBTQ groups exist but cannot obtain legal recognition despite no law barring it, that most LGBTQ people do not disclose publicly, and that LGBTQ people are cast as part of a Western imperialist agenda attributed to two neighbouring powers. Some LGBTQ people have been internally displaced to escape areas whose religious and cultural norms endanger them. Families arrange forced heterosexual marriages, and religious, cultural and familial pressure is used to attempt to convert LGBTQ people, a pressure that falls heavily on younger people. Section 9.9.30(b) of the national aeronautical regulations (2022), issued by the civil aviation ministry, bars competent government agencies from using Passenger Name Record data that reveals an individual's sex life or sexual orientation, alongside racial, ethnic, political, religious, trade-union or health data. The text gives no further detail on entry screening, visa rules, or border checks for trans or LGBTQ travelers.

## DOSSIER 2 (country)

### ASSESSMENT
The country does not criminalise same-sex conduct. Same-sex activity has never been outlawed, including during historic-era colonial rule, and the age of consent is equal for partners of any sex. The absence of a ban is not matched by any protection: a March 2024 stakeholder submission by a local non-governmental organisation and co-signatories to a United Nations human rights review records that the state neither protects nor promotes LGBTIQ+ rights. Public same-sex affection remains exposed to prosecution. Article 176 of the Penal Code punishes anyone who publicly outrages morals by their actions, and local ordinances are applied against LGBTQ people. A travel-safety guide notes there is no explicit national criminalisation while identifying Article 176 and those ordinances as the instruments used against LGBTQ people. No legal gender recognition exists. Trans people have no route to change their identity documents, and the sources record no gender-identity recognition of any kind. Relationship recognition is closed. The constitution has banned marriage between partners of the same sex since 2006, so a trans visitor whose relationship would be locally classed as same-sex has no recognition route, and no other partnership status is available. Violence against LGBTQ people is reported to involve the state's own forces. One travel-safety guide records violence, harassment and torture involving police, security forces and the army. Another reports that armed militia groups in the east of the country have committed extreme violence against LGBTQ people with near-total impunity, and that the collapse of state authority in parts of the country leaves legal protections theoretical. Expression is censored and can carry imprisonment. A travel-safety guide rates the country High Risk for LGBTQ+ travellers. Another advises visitors to keep a low profile and exercise discretion. Church groups promote the view that homosexuality is immoral.

### SUBNATIONAL REGIMES
The record documents one clear subnational difference: in the east of the country, armed militia groups have targeted LGBTQ people with near-total impunity — an assessment the record flags as carrying no incident counts or dates. Elsewhere residents face violence, harassment and torture involving police, security forces and the army, described without geographic limits, and collapsed state authority is noted only in some parts of the country. A separate security-forces report places ordered lethal force against protesters in the capital and two other cities in one earlier period — not an LGBT-specific finding. How the east differs from the rest in degree, and conditions in unmentioned regions, cannot be stated from this record; finer comparison is expectation only (expectation: model knowledge, unevidenced).

- A rights investigation (4 Dec 2017): interviews with former security-force officers and others document security forces recruiting ex-rebel fighters from a neighbouring country and deploying them to use lethal force against protesters in major cities — background on official violence and impunity; no LGBT-specific content.
- A travel guide rates the nation 'High Risk for LGBTQ+': no explicit national criminalisation but Article 176 ('indecent acts') and local ordinances applied to LGBTQ people; armed militia groups commit extreme violence against LGBTQ people with near-total impunity, with collapsing official authority making legal protections theoretical.

### SECONDARY / SITUATIONAL FACTORS

The report records no accountability for the resulting deaths. It is the inventory's only sourced account of how the state's security apparatus treats civilians, and it describes an enforcement environment in which lethal force against civilians goes unpunished.

Enforcement runs through Article 176 of the Penal Code and local ordinances rather than a dedicated anti-homosexuality statute. Residents face violence, harassment and torture involving police, security forces and the army, and in the east of the country armed militia groups have targeted LGBTQ people with near-total impunity. Church groups promote the view that homosexuality is immoral, and LGBT expression is censored with exposure to imprisonment.

## DOSSIER 3 (country)

### ASSESSMENT
The country's penal code penalises "illicit sexual intercourse" and "indecent acts", and the indecent-acts provisions carry up to five years' imprisonment. Same-sex relations are not legal for men or women. The sources identify no provision that criminalises homosexuality by that name; enforcement instead runs through the general morality offences. A media guide published on 21 June 2016 lists the country among the jurisdictions where imprisonment is the penalty and notes that where no specific law exists, other laws are used. A regional advocacy organisation's country review records de-facto criminalisation of gender nonconformity and of the transition process itself, whether social, medical or surgical, enforced through the same indecent-acts and illicit-intercourse provisions. Religious rulings issued in recent years have designated gender-affirming medical steps as impermissible, which narrows the ground on which a trans person can seek or be seen to have undergone care. An international advocacy organisation's country overview, updated 12 March 2026, records that same-sex relations are not legal for men or women, that the indecent-acts provisions carry up to five years' imprisonment, and that no legal protections against discrimination on grounds of sexual orientation or gender identity exist. Same-sex relationships are not legally recognised, and legal gender recognition is unavailable: official identity documents cannot be changed. A visitor whose documents and presentation differ has no domestic procedure to fall back on. Violence against people perceived as gender-nonconforming has been documented in a state/province held by armed groups. An international human rights organisation reported on 8 June 2015 that an armed group killed men accused of homosexuality in areas it controlled, including a city in the east of the country, where a young man was targeted by local Islamists for effeminate dress and "contemporary" clothes; the case dates from November 2014. A development publication reported in June 2015 that Islamist extremists executed three young men accused of homosexual acts in the same city in April 2015, and concluded that conditions for homosexuals in the country were worsening as militias controlled state/provinces. Earlier vigilante violence is also recorded. On 26 November 2012, a news outlet reported that twelve men accused of being gay faced torture and threats of death at the hands of a militia in the country, in the early post-revolutionary period. A 2010 case heard in another jurisdiction concerned a girl in the country who was jailed, raped and returned to her family for a forced marriage after she stated online that she was gay.

### SUBNATIONAL REGIMES
The record documents a geographic axis of enforcement: armed-group territorial control. The documented killings of men accused of homosexuality, in an earlier period, occurred in territory held by armed groups, with one city named in both accounts, and ran through militia and extremist channels rather than the country's courts, a distinct enforcement route from the penal code. Elsewhere the morality-offence exposure is stated nationally with no locality breakdown, and the militia torture of a dozen men in an earlier year carries no location. Reporting from that city in that period is admittedly incomplete, so its apparent prominence is not a measured concentration; where else such violence occurred is expectation (model knowledge, unevidenced).

## DOSSIER 4 (country)

### ASSESSMENT
The country does not criminalise consensual same-sex conduct. The age of consent is 16 and applies identically to same-sex relations. The country has no hate-crime statute and no anti-discrimination law covering gender identity, so violence or discrimination directed at a trans person carries no dedicated penalty or aggravating circumstance. Legality has not prevented police action. In May 2026 local NGOs denounced the detention of around 30 people in a police raid on an LGBTQ+ venue in a city in one state/province. Officers allegedly entered without proper warrants, extorted detainees and accused them of 'committing the crime of homosexuality', a charge with no basis in national law. There is no working legal gender recognition route. A 2017 constitutional action before the national supreme court requires each case to be assessed individually, with certified birth certificates and a psychiatric and psychological report from a specialist demonstrating the veracity of the intended sexual identity, and the court has not granted the requested name and gender changes. The national identity registry announced in September 2016 that transgender people could request an identity card matching their gender identity, but no administrative procedure or mechanism exists to enact the relevant article. A trans visitor cannot expect to obtain documents of the country that match their gender. Partner recognition is partial. Same-sex couples cannot legally adopt children, and the law sets no general healthcare or transition-care provisions. Lesbian couples are permitted to access IVF. In December 2016 the national supreme court allowed a baby to be registered in the civil registry with both mothers' surnames and held that the state must protect all families without distinction. Institutional protection is limited. The national legislature unanimously passed a resolution in May 2016 establishing 17 May as the International Day Against Homophobia, Transphobia and Biphobia; the national supreme court suspended that resolution in August 2016. The country has marked the International Day Against Homophobia since 2000, and the government has begun participating in Pride events. Two candidates were elected Alternate Deputies in the 2015 parliamentary election, the first LGBT members of the national legislature; one had run as the country's first transgender candidate for that legislature. In December 2015 an international human rights organisation declared an openly gay parliamentarian of a political party a prisoner of conscience held in national security custody.

### SUBNATIONAL REGIMES
The record's only located enforcement incident: a police raid on an LGBTQ+ venue in a city in one state/province in a recent period detaining around thirty people, denounced by local human-rights monitors. Everything else — the stalled court cases, absent administrative gender-change procedure, dated national polls, post-election persecution reporting — is stated nationally with no regional breakdown. No other regional variation is documented; whether conditions outside that state differ is expectation only (expectation: model knowledge, unevidenced).

### SOURCE EVIDENCE

- A regional asylum agency country-focus profile (reference period through 2025): a dedicated LGBTIQ+ conditions profile of this nation used for international protection decisions.
- A news report (8 Aug 2015): this nation's first transgender candidate ran for the elected national legislature — a landmark trans political candidacy.

- A blog record (31 Jan 2014): an equal-marriage bill was set to be presented to the elected national legislature that week — a legislative push with no later enactment recorded.
- A news report (13 Dec 2015): an international rights organisation declared the nation's first openly gay parliamentarian a prisoner of conscience held in custody — documented persecution of an openly gay politician.

### SECONDARY / SITUATIONAL FACTORS
Public opinion data is mixed and dated. A poll conducted on 2-3 March 2023 found a majority of residents agreeing that the LGBTIQ population should have the same rights as other citizens; 82% recognised the term LGBTIQ+ and 13% did not, and 36% considered gay, lesbian or bisexual orientation to be innate. A 2023 poll found 48% supported adoption by same-sex couples (34% totally, 14% somewhat) against 39% opposed. A 2013 international survey found 57% of 18-29-year-olds believed homosexuality should be accepted by society, compared with 51% of 30-49-year-olds and 45% of those over 50. Legislative movement on relationship recognition has stalled. A press report on 31 January 2014 said an equal-marriage bill was to be presented to the national legislature that week; no later enactment is recorded. Persecution has tracked the political crisis. Reporting in September 2024 described intensified persecution of LGBTQ residents following a disputed national election, and an opposition presidential candidate left the country on 7 September 2024.

### AFFECTS RESIDENTS MORE THAN VISITORS
For residents seeking legal gender recognition, the 2017 constitutional action before the national supreme court sets an individual assessment standard: certified birth certificates plus a psychiatric and psychological report from a specialist demonstrating the veracity of the intended sexual identity. No name or gender change has been granted under it. Identity documents remain unchanged. The national identity registry's September 2016 announcement that transgender people may request an identity card matching their gender identity has no implementing administrative procedure or mechanism. Family law: same-sex couples cannot legally adopt. The national supreme court ruled on 15 December 2016 that a baby may be registered in the civil registry with both mothers' surnames, basing the decision on a constitutional provision and declaring that the state must protect all families without distinction, so that children and teenagers born into same-sex families enjoy all the rights and guarantees of children born into opposite-sex families. Lesbian couples may access IVF. The law sets no general healthcare or transition-care provisions.

The fate of the equal-marriage bill reported in January 2014 after its presentation to the national legislature is not recorded, and whether the August 2016 suspension of the 17 May resolution remains in force is not sourced. Two reference compilations are catalogued as sources: a regional asylum agency's country focus covering the period through 2025, and an October 2024 LGBTQ guide published by two legal organisations. Their individual findings are not itemised separately here.

## DOSSIER 5 (country)

### ASSESSMENT
Police action is the most serious documented risk to a trans visitor. In September 2017 officers in the capital detained at least 100 people in raids presented as action against prostitution; detainees were beaten, interrogated, subjected to forced medical examinations and blackmailed. A foreign government's annual human-rights report covering that period recorded threats of rape and the use of electric shocks. At least 60 of those held were imprisoned or fined, and the detentions formed part of a wave of arrests ahead of a major international sporting event. Accountability for those arrests has been pursued outside the country. An international human-rights organisation called in October 2017 for an investigation and for releases. The government acknowledged the cases in 2019, and in April 2024 a regional human-rights court issued a ruling on the arrests and the mistreatment. Raiding of private gatherings did not end with 2017. In July 2023 officers entered a private birthday party in the capital, where queer guests were subjected to forced medical examinations, physical and sexual harassment, and threats of outing. Violence against trans people has been met with an inadequate official response. The 2025 annual review of an international LGBTI rights organisation records the murder of a trans woman in the capital in March 2024 and an inadequate police investigation into it. Legal protection is close to absent. Discrimination on the basis of sexual orientation and gender identity is not prohibited. Trans people cannot change the gender marker on official documents, though they may change their name to match their gender identity. A trans visitor whose relationship would be read locally as same-sex has no route to recognition: marriage and civil unions in that category are neither recognised nor performed, and couples in it may not adopt children. An international LGBTI rights organisation has ranked the country last, 49th of 49, in its region every year since 2015, citing a near total absence of legal protection. Hostility is carried by state-affiliated and private media alike. Monitoring by a media-watch project found 77% of 428 media pieces analysed in 2023 to be hateful or biased against LGBTIQ people. In 2024 a state-affiliated television channel aired discriminatory hate speech against LGBTI people after a Women's Day march demanding access to trans healthcare. In 2023 an activist was told to remove a rainbow flag from a cafe in the capital.

### SUBNATIONAL REGIMES
Every documented incident and support structure in the record is capital-located. The raids in one autumn period detaining at least a hundred people, a private-party raid in one summer, a trans woman's murder in one spring with an inadequate police investigation, and a schoolgirl's bullying suicide and the district-court case that followed all took place in the capital. The queer-feminist resource centre, movie nights and magazine likewise operate there, and in one recent year a family transported a young woman to the capital after she came out. The record does not say whether this concentration reflects where events occur or where reporting and services concentrate, and documents no incidents or services outside the capital. Whether conditions differ outside the capital is expectation only (expectation: model knowledge, unevidenced).

### SECONDARY / SITUATIONAL FACTORS
A 2017 report by a foreign government recorded interference with expression, assembly and association through intimidation, incarceration on questionable charges and the blocking of websites, an environment in which the documented raids took place. Media have been criticised for spreading fear and hate toward queer people through discriminatory language, and politicians and officials have used that language to shape public perception. A commentator on a minority-rights webpage criticised this unprofessionalism. LGBTQIA+ media nonetheless exists in the country. An LGBTQ news website, the first of its kind, launched on 25 May 2011, and an LGBTQ online magazine, also the first of its kind, was founded in December 2015 and became an NGO in August 2017. A documentary about a trans man premiered in the capital on 25 November 2017 with a foreign embassy's support and drew about 80 people. The age of consent is 16 for both heterosexual and homosexual sex. By law, no groups are excluded from blood donation apart from people infected with HIV/AIDS. Critics have argued that the country should not be allowed to host international events because of its discrimination against LGBTQIA+ people, a controversy that flared around an international song contest held there in 2012.

### AFFECTS RESIDENTS MORE THAN VISITORS
Discrimination has direct economic effects on trans residents. Trans people are rejected from jobs and housing and pushed into black-market work and precarious housing; one profiled 29-year-old trans woman was arrested, denied housing and employment, and left dependent on community support after her trans-friendly employer closed. A shadow report to a United Nations committee documents state-approved ignorance, employment discrimination, militant homophobia and transphobia in official and private media, forced marriages, and the impossibility of changing identity documents for trans women. The organisation that filed it serves more than 200 LBT people. In 2024 a family forcibly transported an 18-year-old woman to the capital after she came out, according to the 2025 annual review of an international LGBTI rights organisation. The country mandates conscription for able-bodied men aged 18 to 35. Under the regulation on military medical examinations, LGBTQIA+ enlistees can be declared unfit for service under Article 18/b (personality disorders) and Article 17/b (reactive psychoses and neurotic disorders), and queer conscripts often conceal their orientation to avoid hazing. ASSESSMENT
The country's gender identity law has been in force since 27 December 2019. Adults may change their registered name and sex on request to the national civil registry, without therapy or surgery. Civil unions have existed since 2015, and marriage and joint adoption have been open to same-sex couples since March 2022. A trans visitor in a relationship that is read as same-sex under birth-sex status therefore has recourse to marriage. Birth certificates carry a third sex option for intersex children. The 2012 anti-discrimination law prohibits discrimination on grounds of sexual orientation and gender identity and was extended to gender expression in 2018; hate-crime motivation is an aggravating circumstance in criminal proceedings. A bill criminalising incitement to hatred or violence on grounds including sexual orientation, gender identity and gender expression passed the lower legislative chamber on 9 January 2020. The migration and immigration law, enacted 11 April 2021, bans arbitrary discrimination on grounds of sexual orientation and gender identity and extends protection to LGBT immigrants seeking asylum. Article 10 allows complementary protection to be granted to asylum seekers who are not recognised, and a foreigner holding complementary protection may not be expelled or returned to a country where their life, physical integrity or personal freedom is at risk because of, among other grounds, sexual orientation or gender identity. Article 373 of the penal code, which covers offences against morals and good customs, was historically used by police to harass gay men, including for holding hands in public. A 2015 report by a national LGBT organisation recorded that for the first time in seven years no police abuses against LGBT people had been reported, which it credited to the national police force's human rights department. Anti-trans violence is documented and has produced emblematic cases.
