### ASSESSMENT
The country criminalises no conduct on the basis of gender identity or gender expression. Same-sex acts have been legal since the Supreme Court's December 2007 judgment in Sunil Babu Pant v. the national government, which held that homosexuality is not 'unnatural'. The Criminal Codes Act 2017, which replaced the 1963 Muluki Ain, still lists 'unnatural sex' on its face and must be read in light of that ruling; the Muluki Ain criminalised 'unnatural sexual intercourse' with up to one year's imprisonment, and many LGBTQI+ people were arrested under it. The age of consent is 18 regardless of gender or sexual orientation. Legal gender recognition rests on the 2007 Supreme Court ruling recognising the right to self-determined name and gender markers, and a third-gender category has been available on official documents by self-declaration. Government officials limited changes to people identifying as 'third gender' while changes to 'male' or 'female' markers lagged, so the progressive reputation outruns policy. A human-rights organisation called in February 2024 on the government to create a clear, simple, rights-respecting procedure for transgender and third-gender people to obtain official documents, indicating no straightforward document-change process exists; a trans woman photographed that month held a 2023 citizenship certificate listing female gender. In a decision publicly reported in July 2024, the Supreme Court ruled that a trans law student must be legally recognised as a woman on all documents without medical verification, after she filed more than 50 lawsuits beginning in 2021. The decision orders recognition for her alone: others must still petition the court, and the typical medical and surgical requirements persisted for them. Before that ruling, changing markers to 'female' or 'male' had usually required surgery abroad plus in-country medical evaluations including intrusive post-operative genital exams. Local activists assessed in September 2020 that an extensive overhaul of the law and the language of the law is still needed to address inequalities faced by the trans and broader PoMSOGIESC community. Marriage and partnership recognition is partial. Judicial recognition of foreign same-sex marriages exists: a Supreme Court case set a precedent for same-sex spousal visas. On 28 June 2023 a Supreme Court justice issued an interim order directing the government to 'temporarily register' marriages of non-traditional couples and sexual minorities, and the full bench has yet to deliver a final verdict. In April 2024 the national identity and civil registration department instructed local authorities to enter same-sex marriages into a separate register. Temporary registration grants no equal rights: such couples cannot inherit property, receive tax subsidies, make spousal medical decisions or adopt children, and certificates label them 'groom and bride'. The first marriage of a trans woman and a cisgender man took place in November 2023, and as of June 2025 a national LGBT organisation counted at least 17 documented queer marriages. Implementation is inconsistent: a district court rejected a registration in July 2023, and compliance by local governments is unknown. Ground-level conditions for visibly trans people in provincial cities were reported in November 2024 from a city in the southern plains: trans people face discrimination in public spaces and in government institutions and are often subjected to physical and sexual harassment. A 2015 academic study found that despite changing legislation, sexual and gender minorities face deeply rooted stigma and obstacles in virtually every arena of life. Gender-affirming care is limited. The first gender-affirming surgery in the country was performed in June 2025 at a national university teaching hospital, which has since established a Gender Affirming Clinic. Hormone replacement therapy and sex reassignment surgery remain hard to reach: a six-month hormone supply costs 4,500 rupees, providers are few, and many trans residents take hormones without medical supervision because of stigma. Some travel to a neighbouring jurisdiction and to a jurisdiction in the region for surgery. Conversion therapy is practiced, including treatment in rehabilitation centres and traditional exorcism by family members. Lesbian couples are denied IVF, and research and healthcare addressing LGBT residents' needs are severely lacking. A drop-in centre with free HIV testing operates in the capital through a national LGBT organisation with more than 50 branches; three other community organisations also provide resources. The national development programme has recommended that transgender and intersex students be given access to safe toilets and bathroom facilities. Hostility has surfaced alongside the protective framework, though the documented instances are not official government acts: a non-governing political party's protest rally, the elected head of government's reported personal video, and threats made by that leader's supporters. On 30 October 2024 a political party held a protest rally opposing homosexuality and LGBTQ rights, with a community organisation's executive director noting that anti-LGBTQ sentiments are gaining momentum in the country and the subregion. In April 2026 the newly elected head of government faced LGBTQ community criticism over a video containing derogatory and homophobic hate speech; after demands for an apology, supporters markedly increased derogatory comments, hate speech and death threats against the community. A former governing party made homophobic statements during the Civil War, describing homosexuality as 'a production of capitalism' and LGBT people as 'social pollutants'. A 2008 international newspaper report quotes the 'social pollutants' wording as the former rebels' past description of gays and lesbians, and also records that a political party and the party of the former rebels wrote support for gay rights into their platforms — a historical 2008 party-platform change that does not establish the parties' present-day stance and concerned gay rights rather than trans-specific rights. Education and civic space: the national education board has implemented information about sexual and gender diversity in the curriculum for grades 7–9 (ages 13–15), making the country the second in the region to do so, and universities offer courses on LGBT issues. Many LGBT children nonetheless face discrimination and leave school because of threats, bullying and neglect by fellow students and teachers, and transgender residents face severe gender-based violence and interrupted education, especially in rural areas. A national queer youth organisation has filed writ petitions at the Supreme Court demanding legal recognition of gender identity and has advocated for the citizenship bill. During the 2011 census, some enumerators demanded that a child be stripped when parents listed them as 'third gender'. Pride events held annually include a national pride parade (since 2019), a queer womxn pride and a trans pride parade (since 2020).

### SECONDARY / SITUATIONAL FACTORS
Media representation of LGBT people has historically been negative or comic relief, with caricatures of transgender people, gay people and lesbians, though positive depictions include two films released in 2012. Some observers accused media and activists of pinkwashing and spreading disinformation about same-sex marriage and LGBT rights, and national media has been criticised for forcefully using 'third gender' to describe trans men and trans women. The country's progressive international reputation rests on the 2007 ruling allowing self-identified legal gender, which was novel at the time and later reinforced by subsequent cases; the gap between that doctrine and implementation is documented by a February 2024 analysis and by a February 2024 call from a human-rights organisation for a clear document-change procedure.

### AFFECTS RESIDENTS MORE THAN VISITORS
Residents' access to legal gender recognition improved with the Supreme Court's decision, publicly reported in July 2024, that a trans woman be recognised on all documents without medical verification. The ruling applies to her alone: other residents must still petition the court for a 'male'/'female' marker change, where surgery abroad plus in-country medical evaluations including post-operative genital exams remained the usual requirements. Third-gender marker changes by self-declaration had been available earlier, but officials limited changes to that category. Same-sex couples may register marriages under the 28 June 2023 interim order and the April 2024 instruction to enter them in a separate register, but the registration carries no equal rights: no inheritance, no tax subsidies, no spousal medical decision-making, no adoption, and certificates read 'groom and bride'. Compliance by local governments is unknown and a district court rejected a registration in July 2023. A national queer youth organisation has filed writ petitions at the Supreme Court demanding legal recognition of gender identity and has advocated for the citizenship bill. Civil society produced a Gender Identity Draft Bill in October 2021 through a regional transgender network's fellowship, authored by two activists with a legal advocate. Local activists assessed in September 2020 that an extensive overhaul of the law and the language of the law is still needed. Healthcare and support infrastructure: a national university teaching hospital performed the country's first gender-affirming surgery in June 2025 and has established a Gender Affirming Clinic; hormone therapy remains costly (4,500 rupees for a six-month supply) and providers are few, with some residents travelling abroad for surgery. A national LGBT organisation operates a capital-city drop-in centre with free HIV testing and more than 50 branches; three other community organisations also provide resources. Conversion therapy, including rehabilitation-centre treatment and family-organised traditional exorcism, is practiced. Lesbian couples are denied IVF. Education: the national education board has implemented sexual and gender diversity content in grades 7–9 (ages 13–15), and universities offer courses on LGBT issues. LGBT children nonetheless face discrimination, bullying and neglect leading to school dropout, and transgender residents face severe gender-based violence and interrupted education, especially in rural areas. The national development programme has recommended safe toilets and bathroom facilities for transgender and intersex students.

### DELIBERATELY NOT COUNTED
The full bench of the Supreme Court has yet to deliver a final verdict on the 28 June 2023 interim order directing temporary registration of same-sex marriages; the outcome is unresolved in this record. Compliance by local governments with the April 2024 instruction to enter same-sex marriages into a separate register is unknown. The 2011 census incident in which some enumerators demanded a child be stripped when parents listed them as 'third gender' is the most recent census-specific episode documented here; no later census episode is sourced. No trans-specific killing rate with a denominator and period is present in this inventory, so no comparative violence magnitude can be stated; the November 2024 account of discrimination and physical and sexual harassment in a provincial city is the most recent ground-level condition documented.
