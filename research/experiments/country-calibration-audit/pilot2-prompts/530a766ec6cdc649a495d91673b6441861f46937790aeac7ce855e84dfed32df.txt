## DOSSIER A

### ASSESSMENT
The country criminalises no conduct on the basis of gender identity or gender expression. Same-sex acts have been legal since the Supreme Court's December 2007 judgment in Sunil Babu Pant v. the national government, which held that homosexuality is not 'unnatural'. The Criminal Codes Act 2017, which replaced the 1963 Muluki Ain, still lists 'unnatural sex' on its face and must be read in light of that ruling; the Muluki Ain criminalised 'unnatural sexual intercourse' with up to one year's imprisonment, and many LGBTQI+ people were arrested under it. The age of consent is 18 regardless of gender or sexual orientation. Legal gender recognition rests on the 2007 Supreme Court ruling recognising the right to self-determined name and gender markers, and a third-gender category has been available on official documents by self-declaration. Government officials limited changes to people identifying as 'third gender' while changes to 'male' or 'female' markers lagged, so the progressive reputation outruns policy. A human-rights organisation called in February 2024 on the government to create a clear, simple, rights-respecting procedure for transgender and third-gender people to obtain official documents, indicating no straightforward document-change process exists; a trans woman photographed that month held a 2023 citizenship certificate listing female gender. On 30 August 2024 the Supreme Court ruled that a trans law student must be legally recognised as a woman on all documents without medical verification, after she filed more than 50 lawsuits beginning in 2021. Before that ruling, changing markers to 'female' or 'male' had usually required surgery abroad plus in-country medical evaluations including intrusive post-operative genital exams. Local activists assessed in September 2020 that an extensive overhaul of the law and the language of the law is still needed to address inequalities faced by the trans and broader PoMSOGIESC community. Marriage and partnership recognition is partial. Judicial recognition of foreign same-sex marriages exists: a Supreme Court case set a precedent for same-sex spousal visas. On 28 June 2023 a Supreme Court justice issued an interim order directing the government to 'temporarily register' marriages of non-traditional couples and sexual minorities, and the full bench has yet to deliver a final verdict. In April 2024 the national identity and civil registration department instructed local authorities to enter same-sex marriages into a separate register. Temporary registration grants no equal rights: such couples cannot inherit property, receive tax subsidies, make spousal medical decisions or adopt children, and certificates label them 'groom and bride'. The first marriage of a trans woman and a cisgender man took place in November 2023, and as of June 2025 a national LGBT organisation counted at least 17 documented queer marriages. Implementation is inconsistent: a district court rejected a registration in July 2023, and compliance by local governments is unknown. Ground-level conditions for visibly trans people in provincial cities were reported in November 2024 from a city in the southern plains: trans people face discrimination in public spaces and in government institutions and are often subjected to physical and sexual harassment. A 2015 academic study found that despite changing legislation, sexual and gender minorities face deeply rooted stigma and obstacles in virtually every arena of life. Gender-affirming care is limited. The first gender-affirming surgery in the country was performed in June 2025 at a national university teaching hospital, which has since established a Gender Affirming Clinic. Hormone replacement therapy and sex reassignment surgery remain hard to reach: a six-month hormone supply costs 4,500 rupees, providers are few, and many trans residents take hormones without medical supervision because of stigma. Some travel to a neighbouring jurisdiction and to a jurisdiction in the region for surgery. Conversion therapy is practiced, including treatment in rehabilitation centres and traditional exorcism by family members. Lesbian couples are denied IVF, and research and healthcare addressing LGBT residents' needs are severely lacking. A drop-in centre with free HIV testing operates in the capital through a national LGBT organisation with more than 50 branches; three other community organisations also provide resources. The national development programme has recommended that transgender and intersex students be given access to safe toilets and bathroom facilities. Official hostility has surfaced alongside the protective framework. On 30 October 2024 a political party held a protest rally opposing homosexuality and LGBTQ rights, with a community organisation's executive director noting that anti-LGBTQ sentiments are gaining momentum in the country and the subregion. In April 2026 the newly elected head of government faced LGBTQ community criticism over a video containing derogatory and homophobic hate speech; after demands for an apology, supporters markedly increased derogatory comments, hate speech and death threats against the community. A former governing party made homophobic statements during the Civil War, describing homosexuality as 'a production of capitalism' and LGBT people as 'social pollutants'. Education and civic space: the national education board has implemented information about sexual and gender diversity in the curriculum for grades 7–9 (ages 13–15), making the country the second in the region to do so, and universities offer courses on LGBT issues. Many LGBT children nonetheless face discrimination and leave school because of threats, bullying and neglect by fellow students and teachers, and transgender residents face severe gender-based violence and interrupted education, especially in rural areas. A national queer youth organisation has filed writ petitions at the Supreme Court demanding legal recognition of gender identity and has advocated for the citizenship bill. During the 2011 census, some enumerators demanded that a child be stripped when parents listed them as 'third gender'. Pride events held annually include a national pride parade (since 2019), a queer womxn pride and a trans pride parade (since 2020).

### ASSESSMENT
The Gender Recognition Act 2004, in force since 4 April 2005, allows a transgender person to apply to a Gender Recognition Panel for a Gender Recognition Certificate after two years of transition, without mandatory surgery, and to obtain a new birth certificate and full legal recognition of acquired sex for all purposes. The framework followed Goodwin v a neighbouring jurisdiction (2002), in which the international court of human rights found the country in breach of Articles 8 and 12 of the Convention, and reversed Corbett v Corbett (1970), which had barred any change to the sex marker on a birth certificate. Medical gender reassignment is formally approved and available through the national health service or privately, and a December 2002 government policy document states that transsexualism is not a mental illness. Since December 2023 the government no longer accepts gender recognition certificates issued by 50 countries and sub-national jurisdictions, including one neighbouring jurisdiction and one state/province, on the stated ground that those jurisdictions allow people to transition too easily; affected applicants must apply for a domestic GRC instead. The Women and Equalities Minister called the change long overdue. Under the Gender Recognition Act 2004 as originally enacted, a married transgender applicant had to divorce or annul the marriage before a certificate could be issued. Under current law a GRC will not be issued unless the spouse consents, and if the spouse does not consent the marriage must be terminated. Same-sex marriage has been legal across the country since 2014, with the final region following on 13 January 2020. In For Women Scotland v Scottish Ministers [2026] CSOH 59 (Outer House, the top civil court, 19 June 2026), a judge struck down the national prison service's non-statutory guidance that had allowed some trans prisoners to be held in prisons matching their gender identity rather than sex at birth, holding that sex segregation in prisons must rest on a lawful basis. Earlier in the litigation, on 5 February 2026, the court was told that housing trans prisoners by sex at birth would breach human rights and create an unacceptable suicide risk, with government lawyers defending individual risk assessments as flexible. The national transport police amended its strip-searching policy after the April 2025 top-court gender ruling, so that trans women are strip-searched by officers of their sex at birth as an interim position; the prior policy had allowed trans women staff to intimately search women holding a GRC. One state/province police service's interim guidance of 25 June 2025 provides that searches involving removal of more than a jacket, gloves, headgear or footwear are conducted on the basis of biological sex in custody and stop-and-search, though where lived gender differs an officer of the individual's lived gender will be provided on request. One regional police service's searching and biological-sex policy, released under freedom-of-information law, could not be retrieved at research time. New national equality-body guidance on single-sex spaces came into force in August 2026, giving organisations practical examples for compliance; one region, on different equality legislation, is still assessing the top-court ruling's impact. UN human rights experts warned on 18 August 2026 that revisions to the national equality act risk discriminating against and excluding transgender people. A regional rainbow-index assessment for 2026 holds that the country maintains explicit bans or other legal limitations making legal gender recognition practically impossible, and provides hate-crime and hate-speech protection on gender identity only in some regions. The national health service said in March 2024 that gender identity clinics would stop prescribing puberty blockers to children and young people, renewed indefinitely in December 2024 and to be reconsidered in 2027. In April 2025 it was reported that the health service had issued no new prescriptions of gender-affirming hormones to minors in the year since a national clinical review, with some families turning to black-market or imported hormones instead. The review (April 2024) also led to the closure of the national gender identity service for children in March 2024, with only two of eight planned regional centres opened and many 16- and 17-year-olds transferred to adult services. A December 2020 court case found children under 16 could not independently consent to puberty blockers, overturned on appeal in September 2021. Trans hospital patients are to be banned from female- and male-only wards under plans announced by the health secretary at an October 2023 party conference, alongside a commitment that a patient's request for intimate care by someone of the same biological sex will be respected and an update to the health service constitution. The national health department announced a consultation on updates to the constitution to ensure privacy, dignity and safety, with the health secretary stating the changes would recognise different biological needs, criticising diversity spending, ordering a reversal of health service website changes and stopping the health service directing staff to declare pronouns to each new patient. A national trans legal clinic documents the death of Joelle, a 27-year-old trans woman with an aggressive lymphoma: after staff at one NHS trust saw she was a trans woman, she was removed from an available cancer ward bed and placed in a side room, where she spent 16 days deteriorating without cancer pain management, and by the time treatment began the delay was unsurvivable. Her mother states the refusal came the day after the health minister announced plans to block trans women from women's wards. The clinic is pursuing an inquest into national obligations and a clinical negligence claim, and notes that if trans people are blocked from appropriate hospital wards this could become policy across the country. A national trans advocacy organisation's Gendered Spaces Report 2025 surveyed trans, intersex and gender-nonconforming respondents and corroborated their fears of harassment from those opposed to their inclusion in public gendered spaces. Anti-trans rhetoric in national media has been described as increasing and becoming more vitriolic since 2016 and super-charged since 2018, and a 2019 study found numerous press articles from 2018 and 2019 written to cast trans people as unreasonable and aggressive. Regional rainbow-index rankings show the country falling from 1st in the subregion with an 86% score in 2015 to 9th in 2020 with 66% and concern about a hostile climate on trans rights, then 22nd in 2025 with 45%, the third steepest drop in the subregion. A national girls' school trust barred admissions for trans girls without a GRC in January 2022, and in July 2022 the national rugby football unions implemented a single-sex policy for under-12s excluding trans girls from girls' teams. Transgender youth are covered by the national equality act.
