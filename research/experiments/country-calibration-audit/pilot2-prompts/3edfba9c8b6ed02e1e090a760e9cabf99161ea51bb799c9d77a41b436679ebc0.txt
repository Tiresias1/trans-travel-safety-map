The Gender Recognition Act 2004, in force since 4 April 2005, allows a transgender person to apply to a Gender Recognition Panel for a Gender Recognition Certificate after two years of transition, without mandatory surgery, and to obtain a new birth certificate and full legal recognition of acquired sex for all purposes. The framework followed Goodwin v a neighbouring jurisdiction (2002), in which the international court of human rights found the country in breach of Articles 8 and 12 of the Convention, and reversed Corbett v Corbett (1970), which had barred any change to the sex marker on a birth certificate. Medical gender reassignment is formally approved and available through the national health service or privately, and a December 2002 government policy document states that transsexualism is not a mental illness. Since December 2023 the government no longer accepts gender recognition certificates issued by 50 countries and sub-national jurisdictions, including one neighbouring jurisdiction and one state/province, on the stated ground that those jurisdictions allow people to transition too easily; affected applicants must apply for a domestic GRC instead. The Women and Equalities Minister called the change long overdue. Under the Gender Recognition Act 2004 as originally enacted, a married transgender applicant had to divorce or annul the marriage before a certificate could be issued. Under current law a GRC will not be issued unless the spouse consents, and if the spouse does not consent the marriage must be terminated. ### ASSESSMENT

The 8 January 2018 advisory opinion of the regional human-rights court holds that states party to the regional convention must grant same-sex couples access to marriage; the country is a party to the regional system and therefore bound by the ruling, but a lawsuit relying on that opinion to recognise same-sex marriage remains pending and domestic law has not been adapted. A trans visitor in what would be locally classed as a same-sex relationship therefore has no route to recognition of that relationship in the country. There is no broad anti-discrimination law. A November 2015 bill to ban discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity has not advanced since introduction. Limited protections exist in specific contexts: a 2014 law requires that collective dormitory placement for adolescents take into account age, sexual orientation and degree of psychic development; a decree under a 2016 law protects women from violence without discrimination based on sexual orientation; a 2018 law bars discrimination against stateless persons based on sexual orientation and gender identity; a 2020 law treats sexual orientation data as sensitive personal data; and Article 28 of the national public defense ministry's internal rules prohibits discrimination based on sexual orientation against employees.
