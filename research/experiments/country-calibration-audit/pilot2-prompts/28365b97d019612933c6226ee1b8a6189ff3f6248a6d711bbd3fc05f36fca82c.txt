The Gender Recognition Act 2004, in force since 4 April 2005, allows a transgender person to apply to a Gender Recognition Panel for a Gender Recognition Certificate after two years of transition, without mandatory surgery, and to obtain a new birth certificate and full legal recognition of acquired sex for all purposes. The framework followed Goodwin v a neighbouring jurisdiction (2002), in which the international court of human rights found the country in breach of Articles 8 and 12 of the Convention, and reversed Corbett v Corbett (1970), which had barred any change to the sex marker on a birth certificate. Medical gender reassignment is formally approved and available through the national health service or privately, and a December 2002 government policy document states that transsexualism is not a mental illness. Since December 2023 the government no longer accepts gender recognition certificates issued by 50 countries and sub-national jurisdictions, including one neighbouring jurisdiction and one state/province, on the stated ground that those jurisdictions allow people to transition too easily; affected applicants must apply for a domestic GRC instead. The Women and Equalities Minister called the change long overdue. Under the Gender Recognition Act 2004 as originally enacted, a married transgender applicant had to divorce or annul the marriage before a certificate could be issued. Under current law a GRC will not be issued unless the spouse consents, and if the spouse does not consent the marriage must be terminated. Same-sex marriage has been legal across the country since 2014, with the final region following on 13 January 2020. In For Women Scotland v Scottish Ministers [2026] CSOH 59 (Outer House, the top civil court, 19 June 2026), a judge struck down the national prison service's non-statutory guidance that had allowed some trans prisoners to be held in prisons matching their gender identity rather than sex at birth, holding that sex segregation in prisons must rest on a lawful basis. Earlier in the litigation, on 5 February 2026, the court was told that housing trans prisoners by sex at birth would breach human rights and create an unacceptable suicide risk, with government lawyers defending individual risk assessments as flexible. The national transport police amended its strip-searching policy after the April 2025 top-court gender ruling, so that trans women are strip-searched by officers of their sex at birth as an interim position; the prior policy had allowed trans women staff to intimately search women holding a GRC. One state/province police service's interim guidance of 25 June 2025 provides that searches involving removal of more than a jacket, gloves, headgear or footwear are conducted on the basis of biological sex in custody and stop-and-search, though where lived gender differs an officer of the individual's lived gender will be provided on request. One regional police service's searching and biological-sex policy, released under freedom-of-information law, could not be retrieved at research time. New national equality-body guidance on single-sex spaces came into force in August 2026, giving organisations practical examples for compliance; one region, on different equality legislation, is still assessing the top-court ruling's impact. A regional rainbow-index assessment for 2026 holds that the country maintains explicit bans or other legal limitations making legal gender recognition practically impossible, and provides hate-crime and hate-speech protection on gender identity only in some regions. The national health service said in March 2024 that gender identity clinics would stop prescribing puberty blockers to children and young people, renewed indefinitely in December 2024 and to be reconsidered in 2027. In April 2025 it was reported that the health service had issued no new prescriptions of gender-affirming hormones to minors in the year since a national clinical review, with some families turning to black-market or imported hormones instead. The review (April 2024) also led to the closure of the national gender identity service for children in March 2024, with only two of eight planned regional centres opened and many 16- and 17-year-olds transferred to adult services. A December 2020 court case found children under 16 could not independently consent to puberty blockers, overturned on appeal in September 2021. Trans hospital patients are to be banned from female- and male-only wards under plans announced by the health secretary at an October 2023 party conference, alongside a commitment that a patient's request for intimate care by someone of the same biological sex will be respected and an update to the health service constitution. The national health department announced a consultation on updates to the constitution to ensure privacy, dignity and safety, with the health secretary stating the changes would recognise different biological needs, criticising diversity spending, ordering a reversal of health service website changes and stopping the health service directing staff to declare pronouns to each new patient. A national trans legal clinic documents the death of Joelle, a 27-year-old trans woman with an aggressive lymphoma: after staff at one NHS trust saw she was a trans woman, she was removed from an available cancer ward bed and placed in a side room, where she spent 16 days deteriorating without cancer pain management, and by the time treatment began the delay was unsurvivable. Her mother states the refusal came the day after the health minister announced plans to block trans women from women's wards. The clinic is pursuing an inquest into national obligations and a clinical negligence claim, and notes that if trans people are blocked from appropriate hospital wards this could become policy across the country. A national trans advocacy organisation's Gendered Spaces Report 2025 surveyed trans, intersex and gender-nonconforming respondents and corroborated their fears of harassment from those opposed to their inclusion in public gendered spaces. Anti-trans rhetoric in national media has been described as increasing and becoming more vitriolic since 2016 and super-charged since 2018, and a 2019 study found numerous press articles from 2018 and 2019 written to cast trans people as unreasonable and aggressive. Regional rainbow-index rankings show the country falling from 1st in the subregion with an 86% score in 2015 to 9th in 2020 with 66% and concern about a hostile climate on trans rights, then 22nd in 2025 with 45%, the third steepest drop in the subregion. A national girls' school trust barred admissions for trans girls without a GRC in January 2022, and in July 2022 the national rugby football unions implemented a single-sex policy for under-12s excluding trans girls from girls' teams. Transgender youth are covered by the national equality act.
