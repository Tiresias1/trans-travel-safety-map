## DOSSIER A

### ASSESSMENT
Travel documents are the sharpest immediate exposure for a trans visitor to the country. An executive order signed 20 January 2025 redefined sex for national purposes as a male-female binary determined at conception or birth, barred transgender people from government-funded single-sex facilities congruent with their gender identity, prohibited self-selection of gender on passports and visas, and directed the national justice department to "correct the misapplication" of a 2020 Supreme Court employment-discrimination ruling. The same order required national agencies to use "sex" rather than "gender," ordered removal of materials it calls gender ideology, rescinded policies requiring use of names and pronouns consistent with a person's gender identity in national workplaces, prohibited national funding and promotion of gender-identity policies, and rescinded an earlier executive order addressing LGBTQ rights in national prisons. The foreign ministry stopped processing "X"-marker passports and markers not matching sex assigned at birth in February 2025; existing "X"-marker passports remain valid until expiration, and new applications are processed only as M or F per supporting documents. In November 2025 the Supreme Court allowed the passport sex-marker restrictions to take effect, superseding a foreign ministry policy in place since 1992. Trans and nonbinary travelers have reported passports confiscated or blocked at processing, leaving some stranded abroad; a national civil-liberties union sued the foreign ministry on behalf of seven plaintiffs, and the policy remains challenged in national courts. Entry screening carries its own exposure. In February 2025 the foreign ministry announced a ban on trans athletes entering the country to compete in women's sports, flagging visa applicants with an internal marking and treating any applicant listing a gender other than assigned sex as grounds for a permanent ban on grounds of fraud. Multiple foreign governments responded: by March 2025 at least eight governments in one region, including two named neighbouring jurisdictions, had adjusted travel guidelines for trans and nonbinary citizens entering the country, following detentions of citizens from a neighbouring jurisdiction over visa issues; by April 2025 several governments were issuing advisories about trans people travelling here over identity-document and data rules. Facilities access is restricted in a growing number of state/provinces. State/province bathroom bans applying to trans people in K-12 schools and some government buildings are in force in 9 state/provinces and 1 state/province, with outright state/province bans on facility use per gender identity in additional state/provinces; 29 state/provinces, 4 state/provinces and the capital district have no such ban, and some state/provinces make trans bathroom use a criminal offence in certain circumstances. In April 2025 a trans student was arrested and handcuffed in a women's restroom at a state/province capitol in what civil-rights attorneys described as the first known arrest under that state/province's bathroom-restriction law. State/province document rules are tightening. In February 2026 one state/province enacted a law invalidating birth certificates and driver's licences that do not match sex assigned at birth and barring trans people from sex-segregated facilities; rights groups called it a template, and further targeting bills are pending in state/province legislatures. A national equality map sets out which state/provinces still allow gender-marker changes by licence or birth certificate and under what conditions. A national risk assessment dated 13 July 2026 flagged one additional state/province as the latest "Do Not Travel" state/province for trans people, bringing the total to 4 so flagged, and recorded that the 2026 legislative season continued to degrade trans rights while national policies drove nationwide trends. Other national measures in force or in litigation include a military ban executive order, an executive order of 28 January 2025 describing gender-affirming care for minors as "chemical and surgical mutilation of children," an executive order of 29 January 2025 directing law enforcement to investigate schools promoting "gender ideology" and to criminally prosecute teachers who facilitate a trans minor's social transition, including using preferred names or pronouns, a women's sports ban executive order of 5 February 2025, a January 2025 national funding freeze, and an October 2025 rule blocking student loan forgiveness for public workers providing gender-affirming care to transgender youth. Several of these orders have been blocked or enjoined in court. Custody and detention carry documented harm. From 2026 the national corrections bureau adopted one state/province's policies mandating what the source describes as conversion therapy for transgender inmates, including cutting hair short, removing hormone medication, and substituting psychiatric therapy and drugs; imprisoned transgender individuals reported denial of care, and the corrections bureau stopped reporting the number of transgender prisoners held from 2025. In immigration custody, transgender men were reportedly subjected to forced-labour programmes with guards justifying it with statements such as "If you wanna be a man, I'll treat you like a man"; those who refused faced beatings and solitary confinement, and many also reported severe sexual abuse. Official hostility extends to advocacy and speech. In December 2025 the national justice department instructed a national law-enforcement agency to offer cash bounties for information leading to the identification and arrest of transgender activists promoting what it called "radical gender ideology," describing such activists as "domestic terrorist groups." In May 2026 the government's counterterrorism strategy stated that national counterterrorism activities would "prioritize the rapid identification and neutralization of violent secular political groups whose ideology is anti-American, radically pro-transgender, and anarchist." Advocates have compared the administration's actions to the early stages of genocide. Marriage recognition stands on a different footing from the document and facilities regimes. Same-sex marriage is recognized nationwide under a 2015 Supreme Court ruling, and a December 2022 statute repealed the earlier national marriage-defence act and required all state/provinces to recognize existing same-sex marriages; recognition still varies, with same-sex marriages not licensed or recognized in one outlying state/province and status differing across sovereign tribal nations. Employment discrimination on the basis of gender identity is prohibited nationally under a 2020 Supreme Court ruling, and gender-identity hate crimes are punishable under a national hate-crimes statute, though only 23 state/provinces cover gender identity in state/province bias-motivated violence statutes and 37 lacked anti-bias statutes for gender-identity crimes as of 2019. Twenty-two state/provinces plus the capital district outlaw discrimination based on gender identity or expression. Public opinion data from a 2025 poll cited in the sources found 66% of residents supported laws requiring assigned sex at birth to be listed on government documents such as driver's licences and passports (89% of one major party's supporters, 66% of independents, 38% of the other major party's), and 69% supported laws requiring transgender athletes to compete aligned with assigned sex at birth. Gender-affirming care for children is already banned in 27 state/provinces, and in October 2025 proposed national health rules would bar public insurance reimbursement for care to transgender patients under 18.

## DOSSIER B

### ASSESSMENT
The country criminalises no conduct on the basis of gender identity or gender expression. Same-sex acts have been legal since the Supreme Court's December 2007 judgment in Sunil Babu Pant v. the national government, which held that homosexuality is not 'unnatural'. The Criminal Codes Act 2017, which replaced the 1963 Muluki Ain, still lists 'unnatural sex' on its face and must be read in light of that ruling; the Muluki Ain criminalised 'unnatural sexual intercourse' with up to one year's imprisonment, and many LGBTQI+ people were arrested under it. The age of consent is 18 regardless of gender or sexual orientation. Legal gender recognition rests on the 2007 Supreme Court ruling recognising the right to self-determined name and gender markers, and a third-gender category has been available on official documents by self-declaration. Government officials limited changes to people identifying as 'third gender' while changes to 'male' or 'female' markers lagged, so the progressive reputation outruns policy. A human-rights organisation called in February 2024 on the government to create a clear, simple, rights-respecting procedure for transgender and third-gender people to obtain official documents, indicating no straightforward document-change process exists; a trans woman photographed that month held a 2023 citizenship certificate listing female gender. On 30 August 2024 the Supreme Court ruled that a trans law student must be legally recognised as a woman on all documents without medical verification, after she filed more than 50 lawsuits beginning in 2021. Before that ruling, changing markers to 'female' or 'male' had usually required surgery abroad plus in-country medical evaluations including intrusive post-operative genital exams. Local activists assessed in September 2020 that an extensive overhaul of the law and the language of the law is still needed to address inequalities faced by the trans and broader PoMSOGIESC community. Marriage and partnership recognition is partial. Judicial recognition of foreign same-sex marriages exists: a Supreme Court case set a precedent for same-sex spousal visas. On 28 June 2023 a Supreme Court justice issued an interim order directing the government to 'temporarily register' marriages of non-traditional couples and sexual minorities, and the full bench has yet to deliver a final verdict. In April 2024 the national identity and civil registration department instructed local authorities to enter same-sex marriages into a separate register. Temporary registration grants no equal rights: such couples cannot inherit property, receive tax subsidies, make spousal medical decisions or adopt children, and certificates label them 'groom and bride'. The first marriage of a trans woman and a cisgender man took place in November 2023, and as of June 2025 a national LGBT organisation counted at least 17 documented queer marriages. Implementation is inconsistent: a district court rejected a registration in July 2023, and compliance by local governments is unknown. Ground-level conditions for visibly trans people in provincial cities were reported in November 2024 from a city in the southern plains: trans people face discrimination in public spaces and in government institutions and are often subjected to physical and sexual harassment. A 2015 academic study found that despite changing legislation, sexual and gender minorities face deeply rooted stigma and obstacles in virtually every arena of life. Gender-affirming care is limited. The first gender-affirming surgery in the country was performed in June 2025 at a national university teaching hospital, which has since established a Gender Affirming Clinic. Hormone replacement therapy and sex reassignment surgery remain hard to reach: a six-month hormone supply costs 4,500 rupees, providers are few, and many trans residents take hormones without medical supervision because of stigma. Some travel to a neighbouring jurisdiction and to a jurisdiction in the region for surgery. Conversion therapy is practiced, including treatment in rehabilitation centres and traditional exorcism by family members. Lesbian couples are denied IVF, and research and healthcare addressing LGBT residents' needs are severely lacking. A drop-in centre with free HIV testing operates in the capital through a national LGBT organisation with more than 50 branches; three other community organisations also provide resources. The national development programme has recommended that transgender and intersex students be given access to safe toilets and bathroom facilities. Official hostility has surfaced alongside the protective framework. On 30 October 2024 a political party held a protest rally opposing homosexuality and LGBTQ rights, with a community organisation's executive director noting that anti-LGBTQ sentiments are gaining momentum in the country and the subregion. In April 2026 the newly elected head of government faced LGBTQ community criticism over a video containing derogatory and homophobic hate speech; after demands for an apology, supporters markedly increased derogatory comments, hate speech and death threats against the community. A former governing party made homophobic statements during the Civil War, describing homosexuality as 'a production of capitalism' and LGBT people as 'social pollutants'. Education and civic space: the national education board has implemented information about sexual and gender diversity in the curriculum for grades 7–9 (ages 13–15), making the country the second in the region to do so, and universities offer courses on LGBT issues. Many LGBT children nonetheless face discrimination and leave school because of threats, bullying and neglect by fellow students and teachers, and transgender residents face severe gender-based violence and interrupted education, especially in rural areas. A national queer youth organisation has filed writ petitions at the Supreme Court demanding legal recognition of gender identity and has advocated for the citizenship bill. During the 2011 census, some enumerators demanded that a child be stripped when parents listed them as 'third gender'. Pride events held annually include a national pride parade (since 2019), a queer womxn pride and a trans pride parade (since 2020).
