### ASSESSMENT
The country has no legal gender recognition procedure in force. Trans people are not permitted to change their name or gender on official documents, so a trans visitor whose passport marker conflicts with their presentation has no domestic route to alignment. The judiciary has yet to issue final rulings in ten lawsuits filed by trans people seeking recognition of their legal names, among them cases brought by Yren Rotela and Mariana Sepúlveda that have been pending since December 2016. Both plaintiffs have taken communications to the UN Human Rights Committee under the Optional Protocol to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and are awaiting a decision. A 1993 civil court case seeking to register Ramón Fidel, who had undergone gender reassignment surgery, as a woman ended without a decision because Fidel died in a traffic accident before judgment. Two trans migrants were denied identity documents, according to a 2025-26 international human-rights organisation report on the country. The capital's city council banned an event honouring a trans human-rights defender who died in March 2025. Discrimination, harassment and gender-based violence push trans women out of their hometowns into migration and sex work, according to September 2025 press reporting. Since 1992 the Constitution limits marriage, de facto unions and the family to one man and one woman under Articles 49 to 52, and Article 140 of the Civil Code expressly prohibits marriage between persons of the same sex. The 8 January 2018 advisory opinion of the regional human-rights court holds that states party to the regional convention must grant same-sex couples access to marriage; the country is a party to the regional system and therefore bound by the ruling, but a lawsuit relying on that opinion to recognise same-sex marriage remains pending and domestic law has not been adapted. A trans visitor in what would be locally classed as a same-sex relationship therefore has no route to recognition of that relationship in the country. There is no broad anti-discrimination law. A November 2015 bill to ban discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity has not advanced since introduction. Limited protections exist in specific contexts: a 2014 law requires that collective dormitory placement for adolescents take into account age, sexual orientation and degree of psychic development; a decree under a 2016 law protects women from violence without discrimination based on sexual orientation; a 2018 law bars discrimination against stateless persons based on sexual orientation and gender identity; a 2020 law treats sexual orientation data as sensitive personal data; and Article 28 of the national public defense ministry's internal rules prohibits discrimination based on sexual orientation against employees. In October 2016 the national health ministry approved a resolution requiring that trans people may use their social name on medical records, medical history and forms, and that health service officials in the integrated health-service networks must treat trans patients using their identified social name. A 2022 mental-health law, enacted 15 November 2022, provides in Article 3 that mental-health care is governed by non-discrimination on grounds including sexual orientation and gender identity and that in no case may a diagnosis be made solely on the basis of sexual choice, sexual orientation or gender identity. In October 2017 the government implemented a ban on discussion of LGBT issues within public schools, and the Minister of Education stated it might consider burning books that raise awareness about trans people. A 2025-26 international human-rights organisation report records 37 registered feminicides in 2025 and an April 2025 bill to merge the women's ministry into a ministry of the family. Same-sex sexual activity has been legal since 1990, with the age of consent set at 16 for homosexual conduct and 14 for heterosexual conduct. There is no official prohibition preventing entry of gays, lesbians and bisexuals into the Armed Forces and National Police, after the legislature in June 2010 rejected by 42 votes to 4 a bill that would have prohibited entry of homosexuals into the public force. Under the 1997 adoption law, single persons of any sex may adopt regardless of marital status; the law's prohibitions cover only infectious disease, mental disorders or child-abuse records, and equal preference is given to opposite-sex marriages and de facto unions and single women.

## DOSSIER B

### ASSESSMENT
Travel documents are the sharpest immediate exposure for a trans visitor to the country. An executive order signed 20 January 2025 redefined sex for national purposes as a male-female binary determined at conception or birth, barred transgender people from government-funded single-sex facilities congruent with their gender identity, prohibited self-selection of gender on passports and visas, and directed the national justice department to "correct the misapplication" of a 2020 Supreme Court employment-discrimination ruling. The same order required national agencies to use "sex" rather than "gender," ordered removal of materials it calls gender ideology, rescinded policies requiring use of names and pronouns consistent with a person's gender identity in national workplaces, prohibited national funding and promotion of gender-identity policies, and rescinded an earlier executive order addressing LGBTQ rights in national prisons. The foreign ministry stopped processing "X"-marker passports and markers not matching sex assigned at birth in February 2025; existing "X"-marker passports remain valid until expiration, and new applications are processed only as M or F per supporting documents. In November 2025 the Supreme Court allowed the passport sex-marker restrictions to take effect, superseding a foreign ministry policy in place since 1992. Trans and nonbinary travelers have reported passports confiscated or blocked at processing, leaving some stranded abroad; a national civil-liberties union sued the foreign ministry on behalf of seven plaintiffs, and the policy remains challenged in national courts. Entry screening carries its own exposure. In February 2025 the foreign ministry announced a ban on trans athletes entering the country to compete in women's sports, flagging visa applicants with an internal marking and treating any applicant listing a gender other than assigned sex as grounds for a permanent ban on grounds of fraud. Multiple foreign governments responded: by March 2025 at least eight governments in one region, including two named neighbouring jurisdictions, had adjusted travel guidelines for trans and nonbinary citizens entering the country, following detentions of citizens from a neighbouring jurisdiction over visa issues; by April 2025 several governments were issuing advisories about trans people travelling here over identity-document and data rules. Facilities access is restricted in a growing number of state/provinces. State/province bathroom bans applying to trans people in K-12 schools and some government buildings are in force in 9 state/provinces and 1 state/province, with outright state/province bans on facility use per gender identity in additional state/provinces; 29 state/provinces, 4 state/provinces and the capital district have no such ban, and some state/provinces make trans bathroom use a criminal offence in certain circumstances. In April 2025 a trans student was arrested and handcuffed in a women's restroom at a state/province capitol in what civil-rights attorneys described as the first known arrest under that state/province's bathroom-restriction law. State/province document rules are tightening. In February 2026 one state/province enacted a law invalidating birth certificates and driver's licences that do not match sex assigned at birth and barring trans people from sex-segregated facilities; rights groups called it a template, and further targeting bills are pending in state/province legislatures. A national equality map sets out which state/provinces still allow gender-marker changes by licence or birth certificate and under what conditions. A national risk assessment dated 13 July 2026 flagged one additional state/province as the latest "Do Not Travel" state/province for trans people, bringing the total to 4 so flagged, and recorded that the 2026 legislative season continued to degrade trans rights while national policies drove nationwide trends. Other national measures in force or in litigation include a military ban executive order, an executive order of 28 January 2025 describing gender-affirming care for minors as "chemical and surgical mutilation of children," an executive order of 29 January 2025 directing law enforcement to investigate schools promoting "gender ideology" and to criminally prosecute teachers who facilitate a trans minor's social transition, including using preferred names or pronouns, a women's sports ban executive order of 5 February 2025, a January 2025 national funding freeze, and an October 2025 rule blocking student loan forgiveness for public workers providing gender-affirming care to transgender youth. Several of these orders have been blocked or enjoined in court. Custody and detention carry documented harm. From 2026 the national corrections bureau adopted one state/province's policies mandating what the source describes as conversion therapy for transgender inmates, including cutting hair short, removing hormone medication, and substituting psychiatric therapy and drugs; imprisoned transgender individuals reported denial of care, and the corrections bureau stopped reporting the number of transgender prisoners held from 2025. In immigration custody, transgender men were reportedly subjected to forced-labour programmes with guards justifying it with statements such as "If you wanna be a man, I'll treat you like a man"; those who refused faced beatings and solitary confinement, and many also reported severe sexual abuse. Official hostility extends to advocacy and speech. In December 2025 the national justice department instructed a national law-enforcement agency to offer cash bounties for information leading to the identification and arrest of transgender activists promoting what it called "radical gender ideology," describing such activists as "domestic terrorist groups." In May 2026 the government's counterterrorism strategy stated that national counterterrorism activities would "prioritize the rapid identification and neutralization of violent secular political groups whose ideology is anti-American, radically pro-transgender, and anarchist." Advocates have compared the administration's actions to the early stages of genocide. Marriage recognition stands on a different footing from the document and facilities regimes. Same-sex marriage is recognized nationwide under a 2015 Supreme Court ruling, and a December 2022 statute repealed the earlier national marriage-defence act and required all state/provinces to recognize existing same-sex marriages; recognition still varies, with same-sex marriages not licensed or recognized in one outlying state/province and status differing across sovereign tribal nations. Employment discrimination on the basis of gender identity is prohibited nationally under a 2020 Supreme Court ruling, and gender-identity hate crimes are punishable under a national hate-crimes statute, though only 23 state/provinces cover gender identity in state/province bias-motivated violence statutes and 37 lacked anti-bias statutes for gender-identity crimes as of 2019.
