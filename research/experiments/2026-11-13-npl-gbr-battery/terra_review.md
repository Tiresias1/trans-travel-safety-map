## 1. Is the prior US review sound?

Not on the evidence given. **A wide range across divisions does not, by itself, imply that the national score should be near the middle or the mean of that range.** The answer depends on what the country score represents and how national and local risks combine.

The review raises a plausible concern: a flat national assessment could understate safety for visitors who spend time in safer states. But 0.344 is not independently established as accurate merely because it is the divisions’ mean. Equally, **0.264 is defensible only if the national assessment applies the rubric consistently and represents the intended visitor experience**; its consistency with the rubric does not prove it is accurate.

## 2. How to obtain both goals

A principled instrument is possible, but it cannot guarantee the two desired numbers in advance:

1. **Define the national quantity:** for example, safety for an outed trans visitor on a specified itinerary, averaged over a defined distribution of destinations and visitor circumstances.
2. **Assess each destination using the same rubric**, incorporating both national measures and their actual local application, without double-counting them.
3. **Aggregate using weights suited to that quantity**—for example, visitor-destination weights, not automatically equal weights for administrative divisions. Keep a separate range or distribution to show variation.
4. **Check the scale before averaging:** if score differences do not have consistent meaning across the 0–1 scale, the arithmetic mean may not represent average safety.

This could put the US in the 0.3s while retaining a substantial Nepal–UK gap—but only if the underlying assessments support it. The target is a consistently measured comparison, **not preservation of a predetermined gap**. In particular, it is possible that the national framework, rather than the regional mean, misstates one or both countries.
