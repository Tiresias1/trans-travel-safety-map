**(a) The evidence points to a propagation problem, but it does not yet establish that the trade-off disappears.** The 0.344/0.348 difference is explained by different unit sets. The more important issue is whether each division score consistently includes the national exposures applicable to its visitors.

But England’s 0.485 versus the UK’s 0.444 does not, by itself, prove under-propagation. The quoted parent rule applies to a territory that **adds a hostile layer**; a division sharing a UK-wide exposure could still differ because of other conditions. That comparison needs a common definition of the national score and an exposure-level audit.

**(b) Define the national score as the expected destination score for a specified visitor population and trip context:**

\[
S_{\text{country}}=\sum_i w_i S_i
\]

Here, \(S_i\) is the score for visiting division \(i\), including applicable national and local exposures under the same rubric; \(w_i\) is that division’s share of the defined visitor distribution. State whether the unit is trips, visits, or nights, and handle multi-region trips consistently. Include national border exposures and account explicitly for unscored divisions rather than silently dropping them.

Ideally, weights represent trans visitors’ actual destinations. If those data are unavailable, use a transparent proxy such as international visitor trips by destination, report its limitations, and run sensitivity checks with alternative weights. Observed travel may itself reflect avoidance of hostile places.

This definition **could** put the US in the 0.3s and Nepal above the UK, but neither outcome is guaranteed without recalculation. Averaging also assumes the 0–1 scores are comparable enough for differences to have meaningful arithmetic interpretations.

**(c) No.** Enforcing the parent rule alone does not establish Nepal > UK: both countries’ division scores, weights and missing coverage matter. Also, if the parent score is itself the weighted average, requiring *every* division to be at or below it would be mathematically impossible unless they all equal it. Keep the rule specific to divisions with an additional hostile layer, and distinguish a parent-framework benchmark from the calculated national average.

**(d) First, do a paired exposure audit:** for UK and Nepal, trace each relevant national tier-1 exposure into every applicable division dossier and its score, including conditions and consequences of discovery. Verify the claimed UK-wide exposure’s scope rather than assuming identical application. Then recalculate the national scores under the proposed weights.
