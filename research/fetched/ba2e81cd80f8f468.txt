      <h1>
      
        Why Anti-LGBTQI Hate Crimes are not Reported to the Police
      </h1>
      
      
        <div class="vrtx-introduction"><p>What prevents LGBTQI individuals from reporting hate crimes, and what are the experiences of those who do report? A new study uncovers why anti-LGBTI hate crimes are frequently left unreported in Norway.</p>
</div>
      

      
        
      

      
      
      
      
      

      
        
        
          
        

        <div class="vrtx-introduction-image">
          <img src="https://www.sv.uio.no/c-rex/english/news-and-events/right-now/2025/raphael-renter-raphi_cropped.jpg" alt="Rainbow flag" loading="lazy"/>
          
            <div class="vrtx-imagetext">
              
                <div class="vrtx-imagedescription"><p>Photo by Raphael Renter on Unsplash</p>
</div>
              
              
            </div>
          
        </div>
      

      
      

      
      
        <div class="publish-info">
          
      
        <span class="vrtx-authors">
          <span class="vrtx-authors-prefix">By</span>
          
            Kristina Os,
          
            Rune Ellefsen,
          
            Mona Hovland Jakobsen
          
        </span>
      
          
            <div class="vrtx-date-info">
              <span class="published-date-label">Published</span> <span class="published-date">June 3, 2025 </span>
            </div>
          
        </div>
        
        
        

        
      
      
    </div>

    




    <div class="vrtx-article-body">
      <p>Despite Norway’s reputation as an international leader in protecting lesbian, gay, bisexual, trans, queers and intersex (LGBTQI) people’s rights, our <a href="https://www.scup.com/doi/10.18261/njsp.11.1.9">recent study</a><i>&nbsp;</i>reveals major barriers that prevent LGBTQI individuals from reporting hate crimes to the police. &nbsp;We investigated why non-reporting of hate crime is so widespread and found that mistrust in the police, procedural obstacles, and fear of personal costs prevent LGBTQI persons from reporting.</p>

<h2>The experiences of the few who had reported</h2>

<p>In the ongoing&nbsp;<a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20250308040652/https:/www.sv.uio.no/c-rex/english/topics/projects/hatecycle/">HATECYCLE project,</a>&nbsp;we analyzed over 3000 open-ended survey responses from LGBTQI individuals. We found that only 8% of the respondents who had experienced hate crime reported it to the police. Of those who did report, many described discouraging encounters, including cases dismissed despite substantial evidence and instances of unsympathetic or condescending treatment by police officers. Others noted dismissive attitudes or a lack of follow-up, which amplified feelings of neglect. One person said: “I received no help or assistance, no information about where I could possibly get help. I was left feeling that I was not being taken seriously.”</p>

<p>Encouragingly, some positive experiences were also reported. LGBTQI individuals who encountered respectful and understanding police officers felt validated, even when their cases were dismissed, like one person described: “I was received and treated well. Admittedly it was said that nothing further could be done, but it was not the police’s fault.” Such interactions underscore the importance of procedural justice in increasing trust and how in some cases, being taken seriously and treated well by the police is more important for trust than their report having a positive outcome.</p>

<h2>Why most did not report hate incidents</h2>

<p>The study identifies several reasons why LGBTQI individuals avoid reporting hate crimes. A prevalent theme is the belief that reporting “leads nowhere,” like this respondent put to words: “I don’t see the point of reporting when the police never act on hate crimes. Out of my group of 10 queer friends, five have reported one or more incidents, and nothing has been done.”</p>

<p>Many victims perceive police’s failure to act as an inevitability, often reinforced by stories from other LGBTQI persons within their social networks. A significant proportion of respondents also felt that their incidents were not serious enough to warrant police attention, reflecting both potential internalized stigma and uncertainty about what constitutes a reportable hate crime.</p>

<p>The personal costs of reporting further discourage victims. These include fears of retaliation, the emotional toll of recounting traumatic experiences, and concerns about being outed as LGBTQI. For some, these risks outweigh the potential benefits of seeking justice through police reporting. One person explained the reluctance to report: “ʻUnfortunately, I still have a fundamental doubt whether the system cares about me. I can’t bear to put myself in that situation if it only leads to more strain and no other outcome’.”</p>

<h2>A gap between policy intentions and lived LGBTQI experiences</h2>

<p>The findings highlight a profound gap between official policy aspirations to protect LGBTQI citizens and the lived experiences of these individuals. While Norwegian police have taken steps to prioritize hate crime, including establishing a dedicated hate crime unit, the study shows that trust in law enforcement among LGBTQI individuals remains low.</p>

<p>This study offers valuable insights into the barriers LGBTQI individuals face when considering whether to report hate crimes. Findings can thus also be used as a basis for initiating change in the police and beyond to make hate crime victims better supported, and to counteract the widespread lack of trust among LGBTQI citizens in the police’s ability to provide justice and protection in relation to hate crime victimization. Potential measures could include ensuring officers have knowledge concerning LGBTQI-issues through training to improve interactions and reduce bias; considering reporting options outside the police to lower the perceived personal risks and costs of reporting; and public awareness raising efforts to clarify what constitutes a hate crime.</p>

<h2>Alignment with international findings</h2>

<p>The findings of this first study of Norwegian LGBTQI people’s experiences of reporting and reasons for not reporting hate crimes parallel those of numerous international studies: the LGBTQI community has largely negative experiences of reporting to the police and low trust in police officers.&nbsp;The Norwegian respondents’ reasons for not reporting anti-LGBTQI hate incidents are similar to those given by <a href="https://fra.europa.eu/en/publications-and-resources/data-and-maps/2020/lgbti-survey-data-explorer">LGBTQI people in EU countries</a>, where one of the main reasons for not reporting is the perception that police will not or cannot do anything about the matter.</p>

<p>Our findings help explain why a considerable number of hate crimes targeting the Norwegian LGBTQI community are never reported. The lack of trust in the police and criminal justice process is made clear by these findings. Low trust and numerous barriers to reporting makes the dark figures of these crimes substantial. This help illuminate why official statistics on anti-LGBTQI hate crime are not reflective of the experiences of LGBTQI individuals, in Norway and <a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0002764218823844">other countries</a>.</p>

<p>As long as the general view among LGBTQI people is that reporting does not have clear benefits and may involve substantial personal costs, non-reporting is likely to be expected.</p>
