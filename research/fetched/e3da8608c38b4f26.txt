Eren, a 25-year-old art student originally from a small village in Russia, nervously collects&nbsp;his colored-pencil sketches. Eren, who declined to provide his last name due to safety concerns, came to Latvia in 2020 to be with his husband after getting married in Russia, where his gender on official documents was listed as female. There, Eren was completely unable to live openly as a gay, transgender man, but his husband&rsquo;s country offered some relief from Russia&rsquo;s harsh anti-LGBTQ+ laws. Compared with Russia, Eren said, Latvia felt safe. But he feared that if he legally transitioned , he would jeopardize his marriage and immigration status. &ldquo;In Latvia, one gender marriages are not legal,&rdquo; Eren explained through the translator. &ldquo;If I change my documents, my marriage with my husband will be counted as illegal and I won&rsquo;t be able to continue living in Latvia, living with my husband.&rdquo; The recent creation of civil unions in Latvia was a landmark that offers same-sex couples legal rights, but not all the same the legal protections as a marriage. Yet this small Baltic country became the first in the European Union to elect an openly gay head of state in 2023. But the LGBTQ+ community is still fighting to raise its profile and secure legal changes, especially for nonbinary and transgender people like Eren. The centrist coalition that leads the Latvian parliament supported measures to create civil partnerships and after some wrangling, they finally came into force a year ago on July 1, 2024. Irma Kalni&#326;a belongs to New Unity Party, which participates in the three-party coalition. She said that transgender rights had not emerged as a major issue yet in Latvia. &ldquo;We&rsquo;re still dealing with same-sex and LGBT, that&rsquo;s more or less where we are,&rdquo; Kalni&#326;a said. &ldquo;The medications and all that, we don&rsquo;t have that &hellip; No bathroom issues or any of that whatsoever.&rdquo; Ro Vilc&#257;ne, a nonbinary special education teacher, who was a founding board member of Latvia&rsquo;s first transgender non-governmental organization, Transformacija, said members are frustrated at their government&rsquo;s lack of acknowledgement and awareness of their issues. Vilc&#257;ne helped found the group partly out of a desire for legal recognition of nonbinary people. The group was also founded because LGBTQ+ activists saw the need transgender and nonbinary people had for their own community and advocates. This February, Vilc&#257;ne wrote a letter for Transformacija to the Latvian president, Edgars Rink&#275;vi&#269;s, criticizing a comment he made during a TV interview in January. When asked about United States President Donald Trump&rsquo;s recent policy changes targeting transgender people, Rink&#275;vi&#269;s said that at some point discussions around transgender rights &ldquo;went probably too far to one extreme.&rdquo; Vilc&#257;ne was frustrated that Rink&#275;vi&#269;s had not advocated for the LGBTQ+ community as president and as a gay man, and when he did speak on the topic he did not stand with the whole community. Some activists said that despite being a queer person in power, Rink&#275;vi&#269;s was no representative of theirs; he was just a leader who happened to be gay. &ldquo;In our country, so-called &lsquo;discussion&rsquo; often exists rather as threats of physical violence towards people, who can be considered as supporting trans people's rights,&rdquo; Vilc&#257;ne wrote in the letter. Even the structure of the Latvian language has become a bone of contention for some LGBTQ+ activists. The Latvian language &ndash; like Italian, Spanish and numerous other languages &ndash; has masculine and feminine nouns &nbsp;leaving nonbinary people without a 'neutral'&nbsp;option. The Latvian State Language Centre recently decided not to introduce a nonbinary pronoun or word ending in Latvian, instead asserting that the male gendered choice was sufficient, according to Vilc&#257;ne and Latvian inclusive language expert Aiga Veckalne. Vilc&#257;ne had not legally changed their gendered first name because of the cost (Note: Vilc&#257;ne&rsquo;s preferred pronouns are being used here). But they said it can be difficult for nonbinary people to change their names to ungendered ones. According to Vilc&#257;ne, the State Language Centre must approve any unusual name change, and just a few years ago it was not possible. This school year, they asked to be called by their chosen name at work, but no one did. Children asked them, &ldquo;What are you?&rdquo; Krist&#299;ne Garina, the chair of Moza&#299;ka, the first and best-known LGBTQ+ advocacy group in Latvia, said that it is difficult even to bring such issues up with politicians. While a few parties had supported LGBTQ+ rights, none were very vocal about transgender issues. &ldquo;It&rsquo;s like you put oil on fire, everything explodes in the politician&rsquo;s mind,&rdquo; Garina said, running her hands through her short red hair. &ldquo;I would be really afraid to estimate how long it's even going to take.&rdquo; A longtime advocate for the transgender community, Dan Avangard said he believed activism must focus on the community as a whole, not just same-sex marriage. He said this implied that one part of LGBTQ+ was less worthy of human rights than another &mdash; namely the transgender community. Avangard was an activist for five years before stepping back&nbsp;due to exhaustion, he said. When we spoke, he was celebrating personal milestones: his five-year anniversary on testosterone and his upcoming top surgery that week. Despite being worn out from years of activism, he still believed in the ability of LGBTQ+ people to help themselves when institutions fail them. &ldquo;I got sick and tired of fighting,&rdquo; Avangard said. &ldquo;I don't want to spend my energy on convincing people to like me. I want to spend my energy on helping my community, on picking them up when they feel the lowest.&rdquo; He started a small shelter for queer youth in crisis. Avangard saw young community members reaching out for help in a transgender WhatsApp group because some of their parents created unsafe home environments and social services refused to help. Avangard and some friends got a small grant from a Lithuanian organization to renovate a spare room in a community space in R&#299;ga. They organized lend-a-hand events with queer people to make the space liveable for a person in crisis. He said it had been occupied since it opened, and sometimes the need was greater than they could accommodate. &ldquo;We all together made this shelter. We, ourselves, made something for ourselves,&rdquo; Avangard said, exuding pride. They call it Queer Safer Space, and Avangard pays the rent out of his own pocket now. He said he will keep it open as long as he can afford to. The shelter wasn't the only thing Avangard and the transgender community made for themselves. They created the first guide on how to transition in Latvia. Because transgender people were sometimes in legal limbo, the transition process was unclear and varied dramatically by experience. People in the WhatsApp chat pooled their experiences to write their own guide for community members to use. Avangard said that only two laws mentioned transgender people, which left regulations very broad and open to interpretation. For example, one law said that people need a referral from a doctor to get documents changed. There was no guidance on what kind of doctor to see. Avangard joked that you could get one from a dentist. The country lacked specific regulations on what medical changes would qualify a person for legal transition and uniform guidelines for doctors on how to treat gender dysphoria. But transgender people like Avangard were working to fill that gap. &ldquo;Officially, we still don't have any guidance, any laws, nothing,&rdquo; Avangard said. &ldquo;Everything that we have, we created ourselves.&rdquo; Seen a mistake?
