[1] [2] Under French law applicable to French Polynesia, transgender people benefit from protections against discrimination based on gender identity in employment, healthcare, education, goods and services, and housing since 2012, with hate crimes recognized and penalized since 2017; conversion therapies targeting gender identity have been banned since 2022. [1] Legal gender recognition is available without restrictions, enabling changes to legal gender and name, while gender-affirming care such as hormone therapy is permitted for adults and, with limitations, for minors. [3] Culturally, Tahiti's Polynesian heritage recognizes māhū—individuals embodying both male and female spirits in traditional roles like caregiving and cultural preservation—as accepted figures predating colonization, though modern transfeminine identities like rae-rae, often involving hormone use or surgery, face varying degrees of stigma influenced by Christian missionary legacies and urbanization. [4] [2] Despite these advancements, challenges persist, including reports of transphobic violence and aggression in Tahiti, limited enforcement in rural areas, and economic pressures leading some rae-rae to sex work due to employment discrimination. [1] [4] Local advocacy groups like Cousins Cousines in Tahiti promote visibility through events such as Pride Week and Transgender Visibility Day, highlighting ongoing efforts to address gaps in societal acceptance and constitutional protections specific to gender identity. [1] Overall, transgender rights in Tahiti reflect a progressive legal foundation tempered by cultural fluidity and residual social hurdles. [2] Cultural Context Māhū Identity In traditional Polynesian culture, māhū are individuals assigned male at birth who embody both masculine and feminine spirits, occupying a distinct third-gender position that transcends binary categories. [2] [5] This identity, translating roughly to "in the middle," integrates spiritual significance with social functions, where māhū were viewed as possessing unique insights from dual perspectives, often serving as mediators or advisors in community matters. [5] [6] Pre-colonial Tahitian society historically accepted māhū without stigma, assigning them revered roles in family caregiving, artistic performances, and communal rituals. [7] They frequently acted as teachers of traditional dances, performing sacred roles typically reserved for women, such as embodying goddesses in temple ceremonies inaccessible to females. [7] [5] This integration highlighted their cultural value, as māhū contributed to preserving knowledge and fostering social harmony through their blended gender expression. [2] The māhū identity persists in contemporary Tahitian society, maintaining its distinction from strict male-female binaries as a culturally embedded non-binary role rather than a medicalized condition. [7] [8] Rae-rae Identity The rae-rae identity emerged in Tahiti during the 1960s among individuals rooted in māhū traditions who began adopting a more overt feminine presentation, distinguishing themselves through full embodiment of female gender roles. [7] [9] Unlike the more integrated cultural role of māhū, rae-rae are often linked to urban environments, with many pursuing medical transitions such as hormone therapy and surgeries to align their bodies with their gender identity, and some engaging in sex work, which contributes to varied social views portraying them as more provocative or marginalized compared to traditional third-gender figures. [10] [7] Rae-rae self-identify as women assigned male at birth, representing a modern evolution of transfeminine expression in Polynesian society that emphasizes personal transition over liminal cultural acceptance. [8] [2] Legal Framework Gender Recognition In French Polynesia, including Tahiti, transgender individuals have the legal right to change their gender marker with no restrictions, as determined by applicable French laws. [3] The procedure for modifying the sex mention on civil status records is handled judicially, requiring applicants to demonstrate a discrepancy between their birth-assigned sex and identified gender before a tribunal. [11] Name changes accompany gender recognition as a matter of right, integrated into the civil registry process under French oversight to ensure consistency in official records. [12] Approved modifications propagate to essential documents, including national identity cards and passports, facilitating alignment of legal identity with personal gender presentation across administrative functions. [11] Anti-Discrimination Protections Transgender individuals in Tahiti benefit from French anti-discrimination laws applicable to French Polynesia, which prohibit bias based on gender identity in employment, housing, and access to goods and services. [2] These statutes, enforced through the French Penal Code and equality frameworks, extend protections to scenarios such as workplace transitions, barring employers from adverse actions like termination or denial of promotion due to an employee's gender expression or identity change. [3] Hate crimes and incitement motivated by gender identity are criminalized, with aggravated penalties under national law for offenses targeting transgender persons. [13] While enforcement relies on French judicial oversight, these measures align with broader equality principles without requiring prior gender recognition for invocation. [8] Healthcare Access Transition Services Transgender individuals in French Polynesia can access hormone replacement therapy (HRT) by consulting a primary care physician, who prescribes hormones tailored to the person's physiology. [14] However, these treatments often incur high costs locally, prompting discussions for expanded government coverage of HRT. [14] [15] As part of France's healthcare system, transition care encompassing HRT and related medical interventions is permitted for adults in French Polynesia, with social security provisions applying through the territory's Caisse de Prévoyance Sociale, though specialized surgical options may necessitate referral to facilities in metropolitan France and are not covered by local CPS. [3] Residents typically handle logistics via medical referrals, potentially involving travel for advanced procedures not available on-island. [16] Mental Health Support Mental health support for transgender individuals in Tahiti is available through general psychological services within French Polynesia's healthcare system, though a study of gender diverse people found that only 26% had ever consulted a therapist. [4] Cultural elements, such as the traditional acceptance of māhū roles in family and community activities like dance and caregiving, provide informal identity affirmation that acts as a protective factor against stigma and fosters a sense of belonging. [4] Organizations like Cousins Cousines de Tahiti offer community-based support for LGBTQ+ individuals, potentially including counseling resources. [17] Challenges persist due to limited local expertise in gender-specific care, with some transgender people, particularly raerae, experiencing psychological distress from familial rejection and migrating to mainland France for better access to specialized services. [2] Social Dynamics Public Acceptance Societal acceptance of gender-diverse individuals in Tahiti, particularly those embodying the māhū role, is influenced by longstanding Polynesian traditions that recognize gender diversity, with māhū embodying both male and female qualities and holding respected positions in community and cultural life. [18] This cultural integration fosters visibility for māhū in media, professions, and traditions, where they participate openly without widespread ostracism. [8] In tourism and everyday interactions, gender diversity associated with māhū is perceived positively, with Tahiti promoted as a welcoming destination that highlights its inclusive heritage to attract LGBTQ+ visitors, reflecting broader public tolerance in social settings. [10] Familial and community support often aligns with these traditions, as māhū individuals report integration and acceptance within families, underscoring a societal framework that values gender liminality. [2] Discrimination Challenges Despite the cultural recognition of third-gender identities, rae-rae individuals in Tahiti often encounter stigma linked to stereotypes of sex work and urban marginalization. [2] [10] This perception persists as rae-rae are sometimes viewed as deviating from traditional māhū roles, leading to social exclusion in city settings. [4] Family rejection remains a significant issue for many rae-rae, exacerbating isolation despite broader societal tolerance for gender diversity. [2] Employment discrimination further compounds these challenges, with rae-rae facing barriers to job opportunities that legal protections have not fully addressed. [2] Discrimination appears more pronounced in urban areas compared to rural ones, highlighting enforcement gaps and uneven social dynamics across Tahiti.
