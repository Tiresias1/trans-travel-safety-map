            <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-body field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><h3>1. Introduction</h3>
<p>The <em>Code</em> states that it is public policy in Ontario to recognize the inherent dignity and worth of every person and to provide for equal rights and opportunities without discrimination. The provisions of the <em>Code</em> are aimed at creating a climate of understanding and mutual respect for the dignity and worth of each person, so that each person feels a part of the community and feels able to contribute to the community.</p>
<p>Every person in Ontario has a right to be free from discrimination and harassment relating to sexual orientation in the all the social areas protected by the <em>Code</em>. These include employment, services, goods and facilities, housing accommodation, contracts and membership in trade unions and vocational associations.</p>
<h4>1.1 Sexual orientation</h4>
<p>“Sexual orientation” is not specifically defined in the <em>Code</em>. However, the OHRC recognizes that sexual orientation is more than simply a “status” that an individual possesses; it is an immutable personal characteristic that forms part of an individual’s core identity. Sexual orientation encompasses the range of human sexuality from gay and lesbian to bisexual and heterosexual orientations, including intimate emotional and romantic attachments and relationships. It is most commonly gay and lesbian people who make claims of discrimination on the basis of sexual orientation. However, the protection of the <em>Code</em> extends to all individuals who are denied equal treatment because of sexual orientation. The <em>Code </em>also prohibits discrimination because of gender identity, such as that faced by transsexual, transgender and intersex persons. These protections are extended on the basis of sex rather than sexual orientation, and are discussed in the OHRC’s <a href="/en/resources/Policies/PolicyGenderIdent">Policy on discrimination and harassment because of gender identity</a>.</p>
<p><span style="line-height: 1.538em;">Courts at all levels have recognized the past and ongoing disadvantage suffered by lesbian, bisexual and gay people.</span><sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn1" name="fnB1">[1]</a></sup><span style="line-height: 1.538em;"> In </span><em style="line-height: 1.538em;">Egan v. Canada</em><span style="line-height: 1.538em;">, the Supreme Court noted:</span></p>
<blockquote>
	<p>The historic disadvantage suffered by homosexual persons has been widely recognized and documented. Public harassment and verbal abuse of homosexual individuals is not uncommon. Homosexual women and men have been the victims of crimes of violence directed at them specifically because of their sexual orientation. They have been discriminated against in their employment and their access to services.</p>
	<p>They have been excluded from some aspects of public life solely because of their sexual orientation. The stigmatization of homosexual persons and the hatred which some members of the public have expressed towards them has forced many homosexuals to conceal their orientation. This imposes its own associated costs in the work place, the community and in private lif<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn2" name="fnB2">[2]</a></sup>.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>While our province’s statutes prohibit discrimination and harassment on the basis of sexual orientation, unfair treatment based in homophobia and heterosexism is widespread and even socially accepted among many people. The <em>Code</em> requires that the OHRC and all organizations under its mandate take steps to prevent and appropriately respond to this unfair treatment, and develop a culture of rights that<br>
	is inclusive of lesbian, bisexual and gay people.</p>
<h4>1.2 Homophobia and heterosexism</h4>
<p>“Homophobia” and “heterosexism” are terms used to describe prejudice relating to sexual orientation. They refer to the assumption that heterosexuality is superior and preferable, and is the only right, normal or moral expression of sexuality. Both may also be the basis for negative treatment of gay, lesbian or bisexual people based on sexual orientation. Although these terms are closely related and overlapping, they also can emphasize different aspects and expressions of prejudice, and can therefore be helpful in identifying and addressing different aspects of the discrimination and harassment experienced by lesbian, bisexual or gay people.</p>
<p>“Homophobia” is often defined as the irrational aversion to, or fear or hatred of gay, lesbian or bisexual people and communities, or to behaviours stereotyped as “homosexual.” It is commonly used to signify a hostile psychological state in the context of overt discrimination, harassmetn or violence against gay, lesbian or bisexual people.</p>
<p>“Heterosexism” refers to the assumption that everyone is heterosexual. This definition is often used in the context of discrimination against bisexual, lesbian and gay people that is less overt, and which may be unintentional and unrecognized by the person or organization responsible for the discrimination. It can also be useful in understanding and identifying some kinds of institutional or societal bias, although homophobia may also be at play.</p>
<h4>1.3 A further word about terminology</h4>
<p>Terminology is fluid, and what is considered appropriate tends to evolve over time. Moreover, people within a group may prefer different terms to describe themselves. However, it is useful to identify terms that are considered most appropriate to avoid compounding a person’s experience of prejudice, harassment, or discrimination. It is generally best to use terms by which individuals self-identify, such as “bisexual,” “gay,” “lesbian” and “two-spirit.”</p>
<p>In contemporary usage, “gay” usually refers to men, although it is also used as a general term instead of “homosexual.” Some women may identify as “gay,” but may prefer the term “lesbian,” which refers specifically to women. Aboriginal lesbian, bisexual, or gay people may describe themselves as “two-spirit” or “two-spirited.”<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn3" name="fnB3">[3]</a></sup> Individuals may use other terms to describe their sexual orientation; however “gay,” “lesbian,” and “bisexual” are usually accepted as neutral, general terms.</p>
<p>The term “homosexual” was popularized through medical usage, and has often been used to denigrate and stereotype lesbian and gay people, as well as a range of behaviours and practices ascribed to them. It is sometimes used as a general term, such as in legal documents and medical texts, and some individuals may identify as “homosexual.” However, many lesbian and gay people may perceive the term to be clinical or offensive, and bisexual people may also perceive it as exclusionary. It is therefore often better to avoid using the term “homosexual,” particularly to refer to an individual, and to use instead the terms by which people self-identify, such as “bisexual,” “lesbian” and “gay.”</p>
<h4>1.4 The purpose and scope of the policy</h4>
<p>This policy sets out the position of the OHRC with respect to sexual orientation at the time of publication, and replaces the OHRC’s earlier policy, approved in January 2000. The policy was developed based on extensive research and community consultations, and was updated in 2006 to reflect the significant legal and legislative changes that took place after the initial document was approved.<br>
	This policy deals primarily with issues that could form the basis of a human rights claim of discrimination. The policy is therefore bounded by the provisions of the <em>Code</em> and Canada’s legal framework for analyzing discrimination. At the same time, the policy interprets the protections in the <em>Code</em> in a broad and purposive manner. This is consistent with the principle that the quasi-constitutional status of the <em>Code</em> requires that it be given a liberal interpretation that best ensures its anti-discriminatory goals are attained.<br>
	OHRC policy statements contribute to creating a culture of human rights in Ontario. This policy is intended to help the public understand <em>Code</em> protections against discrimination and harassment because of sexual orientation. It is also meant to assist individuals, employers, organizations, providers of services and housing accommodation, and policy makers in understanding their responsibilities and acting appropriately to ensure compliance with the <em>Code.</em></p>
<h3>2. International protections</h3>
<p>Several international bodies and covenants recognize the rights of gay, lesbian and bisexual people, and the need for protection against discrimination and violence based on sexual orientation.</p>
<p>The United Nations <em>International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights</em> (1966) (the <em>Covenant</em>) is monitored by the United Nations Human Rights Committee (UNHRC). The <em>Covenant</em> does not specifically list sexual orientation as a protected ground; however, in 1994, the UNHRC held that laws criminalizing consensual homosexual conduct violate protections for privacy (article 17) and against discrimination (articles 2 and 26),<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn4" name="fnB4">[4]</a></sup> and have since affirmed the rights of same-sex couples.<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn5" name="fnB5">[5]</a></sup> A number of UN bodies and documents explicitly list sexual orientation in non-discrimination and protections clauses.<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn6" name="fnB6">[6]</a></sup></p>
<p>The<em> European Convention for the Protection of Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms</em> (ECHR) has been interpreted to extend protections on the basis of sexual orientation. Although the ECHR does not explicitly include sexual orientation in its prohibition on discrimination (Article 14), the European Court of Human Rights has ruled that discrimination and privacy (Article 8) protections extend to sexual orientation and the family relationships of lesbian and gay people.<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn7" name="fnB7">[7]</a></sup> The European Union (EU) has also established important protections based on sexual orientation.<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn8" name="fnB8">[8]</a></sup></p>
<h3>3. The Canadian context</h3>
<h4>3.1 Sexual orientation in provincial and federal human rights protections</h4>
<p>The OHRC first recommended in 1977 that the <em>Code</em> should extend protection from discrimination on the basis of sexual orientation.<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn9" name="fnB9">[9]</a></sup> The Ontario legislature amended the <em>Code</em> to add the ground of sexual orientation in 1986. In 1996, the OHRC initiated a community consultation process, resulting in an options paper<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn10" name="fnB10">[10]</a></sup> and increased efforts in education, enforcement, complaint processing and litigation relating to sexual orientation. Out of this process, the OHRC developed the <em>Policy on Discrimination and Harassment because of Sexual Orientation</em>, approved in January 2000.</p>
<p>On June 20, 1996, section 3(1) of the <em>Canadian Human Rights Act </em>(CHRA) was amended to include sexual orientation as a protected ground. Administered by the Canadian Human Rights Commission, the CHRA guarantees the right to equality, equal opportunity, fair treatment and an environment free from discrimination in employment and the provision of goods, services, facilities or accommodation within federal jurisdiction.</p>
<p>The ground of sexual orientation is not named in the anti-discrimination section of the <em>Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms</em> (the <em>Charter</em>: section 15).<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn11" name="fnB11">[11]</a></sup> However, the courts have accepted that section 15 is to be interpreted broadly, and that “analogous” grounds, i.e., personal characteristics other than those specifically listed, may also form the basis for discrimination against a group or individual.<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn12" name="fnB12">[12]</a></sup> In 1995, in <em>Egan </em>v.<em> Canada</em>,<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn13" name="fnB13">[13]</a></sup> noting the significant historical and ongoing disadvantage faced by gay and lesbian people, the Supreme Court unanimously found that sexual orientation was an analogous ground similar to the other grounds in section 15.</p>
<p>The Supreme Court subsequently found, in <em>Vriend v. Alberta </em>(1998),<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn14" name="fnB14">[14]</a></sup> that the Alberta government’s assertion that human rights protection was being introduced incrementally did not justify the failure to include protection for lesbians and gay men in the province’s human rights legislation, and that the omission of this protection is not reasonable within the meaning of section 1 of the <em>Charter</em>. The Court therefore required that sexual orientation be “read into” the offending legislation, and that Alberta extend protection from discrimination based on sexual orientation. All jurisdictions in Canada now offer protections from discrimination on the basis of sexual orientation in their human rights legislation.</p>
<h4>3.2 Recognition of same-sex relationships</h4>
<p>Despite legal protections on the basis of sexual orientation, same-sex couples have been subject to specific kinds of discrimination because their relationships were not recognized under the law. Statutes have traditionally used the concept of “spouse” as the basis for allocating rights, powers, benefits and responsibilities to partners, and “spouse” has been implicitly or explicitly defined in heterosexual terms. This was reflected in many Ontario statutes, including the <em>Code</em>; however, there have been significant changes in legislation and case law on these matters in recent years.</p>
<p>Same-sex relationships began to gain important recognition through cases involving family law, and denial of spousal survivor, pension, health and employment benefits. In these cases, definitions of spouse and marital status that were limited to opposite-sex couples were found by human rights tribunals to be discriminatory<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn15" name="fnB15">[15]</a></sup> and to constitute denial of the equality rights in section 15 of the <em>Charter</em>.<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn16" name="fnB16">[16]</a></sup> In <em>M. v. H</em>, (1999),<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn17" name="fnB17">[17]</a></sup> the Supreme Court of Canada found that opposite-sex definition of “spouse” in section 29 of Ontario’s <em>Family Law Act</em><sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn18" name="fnB18">[18]</a></sup> (<em>FLA</em>), which precluded “M” from making an application for support from her former same-sex partner of 12 years, violated section 15 of the <em>Charter</em>:</p>
<blockquote>
	<p>The societal significance of the benefit conferred by the statute cannot be overemphasized. The exclusion of same-sex partners from the benefits of s. 29 of the FLA promotes the view that M., and individuals in same-sex relationships generally, are less worthy of recognition and protection. It implies that they are judged to be incapable of forming intimate relationships of economic interdependence as compared to opposite-sex couples, without regard to their actual circumstances.<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn19" name="fnB19">[19]</a></sup></p>
</blockquote>
<p>In response, the Ontario legislature amended the <em>FLA </em>and a number of other statutes so that they applied to same-sex partners, adding “same-sex partnership status” as a prohibited ground of discrimination under the <em>Code</em>.</p>
<p>While these measures were important in extending greater equality rights, there were still many distinctions in law that had discriminatory effects on gay, lesbian and bisexual people and their families. Some of these distinctions related to access to the actual status of marriage. While opposite-sex couples continued to have the choice to live “common-law” or to become married, those in same-sex couples were still denied this choice. Furthermore, the insistence on a separate category of same-sex partnership, instead of inclusive definitions of terms such as “marital status,” “family,” “spouse,” and “relative” reaffirmed discriminatory views and reinforced the stereotypes held by some, that same sex couples are not “real” families.<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn20" name="fnB20">[20]</a></sup></p>
<p>This situation shifted significantly on June 10, 2003, when the Ontario Court of Appeal released its decision in <em>Halpern v. Attorney General of Canada</em> (“<em>Halpern</em>”).<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn21" name="fnB21">[21]</a></sup> In its decision, the Court found that the common law definition limiting marriage to persons of the opposite-sex breached the equality rights of same-sex couples under the <em>Charter</em>. The Court reformulated the definition of marriage as “the voluntary union for life of two persons to the exclusion of all others,” effective immediately. Following this decision, the Government of Canada announced that it would not pursue appeals in <em>Halpern </em>and similar cases in other provinces.<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn22" name="fnB22">[22]</a></sup> As a result of <em>Halpern</em>, Ontario became the first jurisdiction in Canada in which same-sex couples could legally marry. Many other jurisdictions followed suit,<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn23" name="fnB23">[23]</a></sup> and the federal government began a process of addressing the matter.<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn24" name="fnB24">[24]</a></sup></p>
<p>On March 9, 2005, the Ontario Legislature passed legislation amending a broad range of provincial statutes, changing definitions of spouse and marriage and removing other heterosexist bias.<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn25" name="fnB25">[25]</a></sup> Changes to the <em>Code</em> included removal of the ground of “same-sex partnership,” which was no longer required due to the newly inclusive, gender-neutral definitions of “marital status” and “spouse.”</p>
<p>On July 20, 2005, the federal <em>Civil Marriage Act</em> was signed into law, legalizing same-sex marriage across Canada by defining civil marriage as “the lawful union of two persons to the exclusion of all others.”<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn26" name="fnB26">[26]</a></sup> Amendments were made to federal legislation most directly affected by the <em>Civil Marriage Act</em>, replacing opposite-sex definitions of “spouse” and other language with gender-neutral terms. Additional barriers were removed by changing legislative references to “natural” parents and “blood” or adoptive relationships, to focus instead on “legal” parent-child and familial relationships.<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn27" name="fnB27">[27]</a></sup></p>
<h4>3.3 A note on balancing rights</h4>
<p>A question that has arisen in a number of cases alleging discrimination based on sexual orientation is that of when parties make competing rights claims.<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn28" name="fnB28">[28]</a></sup> It should be noted that the extension of rights to one group does not itself diminish the rights of another group, and it must be established whether there is indeed a genuine conflict. If so, rights claims have been balanced based on the particulars of each case, and the understanding that no right is absolute.<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn29" name="fnB29">[29]</a></sup> Case law has indicated that services normally offered to the public must be offered in a non-discriminatory manner,<sup><a href="/en/policy-discrimination-and-harassment-because-sexual-orientation/part-i-%E2%80%93-context-sexual-orientation-human-rights-protections-case-law-and-legislation#fn30" name="fnB30">[30]</a></sup> and that human rights protections are to be interpreted broadly, while defences for discrimination are interpreted narrowly.</p>
