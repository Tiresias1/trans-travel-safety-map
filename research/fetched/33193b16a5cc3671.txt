<body>
 <div>In a landmark ruling, the Court of Appeal has ordered that a residence permit be issued to Richard Sankar’s same-sex spouse, Timothy Haymon, declaring that the refusal of the permit based on sexual orientation contravened fundamental constitutional rights.</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>While the decision appears to be a significant step forward for LGBTQ+ rights in the territory, the appeals court stopped short of ordering changes to the territory’s marriage laws. </div>
 <div> </div>
 <div>Court’s findings </div>
 <div>Delivering its written reasons on October 27, the Court of Appeal – Justice Turner, Justice Cornelius Thorne, and Justice Hylton – upheld key findings of the trial judge, Anthony Gruchot, while advancing crucial clarification on the scope of constitutional protections.</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>They ordered that a residence permit be issued. The court allowed the couple’s appeal in part, ordering the Director of Immigration to issue a residence permit to Haymon as the spouse of Sankar. </div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>The court held that the term ‘spouse’ in the Immigration Ordinance must be construed in light of constitutional guarantees to include a same-sex spouse lawfully married in another jurisdiction.</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>It also issued a declaration of rights violations. The court declared that the Director’s refusal letter of October 1, 2021, interfered with Haymon’s fundamental rights to equality before the law (section 7), protection from discrimination (section 16), and private and family life (section 9).</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>Notably, no order was given to amend the territory’s marriage law. </div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>The court declined to order amendments to the Immigration or Marriage Ordinance, citing the Constitution’s express definition of marriage as between a man and a woman (section 10) as a lex specialis – meaning more general rights could not override this explicit provision. </div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>The court emphasised that while marriage law remained unchanged, the Constitution required equal treatment in the context of immigration benefits.</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>Another notable finding of the court was that there was no error in the trial judge’s decision to award only 50% of costs to the appellants, given that they did not succeed on all grounds, particularly on legislative amendment and full recognition of same-sex marriage.</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>Background</div>
 <div>The proceedings arose from three consolidated appeals brought before the Court of Appeal, involving Timothy Haymon, a US national, and Richard Sankar, a naturalised Turks and Caicos Islander. </div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>The couple married in Florida in 2020, where same-sex marriage is legally recognised. In August 2021, Haymon applied for a residence permit under the Immigration Ordinance, which would have allowed him to live and work in the TCI alongside his husband. At the time, he held only a homeowner’s permit, which did not grant him employment rights.</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>The Director of Immigration denied the application, explaining in a letter dated October 1, 2021, that, in the absence of a statutory definition of ‘spouse’, the authorities relied on the Marriage Ordinance and the Constitution, both of which were interpreted as limiting marriage to unions between a man and a woman. </div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>The letter further stated that neither same-sex marriage nor civil unions were recognised in the territory, and that any change would be a matter for government policy.</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>Haymon and Sankar challenged the refusal, arguing that it breached their constitutional rights to equality and non-discrimination. </div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>The matter escalated through the courts, culminating in three appeals: one brought by Haymon and Sankar challenging the refusal and seeking both a permit and legislative reform, one brought by the government contesting the trial judge’s declarations of unconstitutionality, and a third regarding the allocation of legal costs.</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>Legal arguments</div>
 <div>Represented by counsels Tim Prudhoe and Nadia Chiesa, both of Stanbrook Prudhoe and supported by intervenors Colours Caribbean, the couple contended that the refusal of the residence permit on the basis of sexual orientation violated their rights under sections 7, 9, and 16 of the Constitution – provisions safeguarding equality before the law, protection of private and family life, and protection from discrimination. </div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>They urged the court to interpret ‘spouse’ in the Immigration Ordinance purposively, reflecting the evolving understanding of family and the principles of equality and non-discrimination. They argued for the recognition of foreign same-sex marriages for immigration purposes.</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>The government – represented by the Clemar Hippolyte of the Attorney General’s Chambers and King’s Counsel Ivan Hare, UK counsel – maintained that the Constitution expressly restricts marriage to opposite-sex couples (section 10), and that neither the Immigration nor Marriage Ordinances sanctioned recognition of same-sex marriages. </div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>It argued that the court should not bypass clear constitutional limits through broad interpretation and that any change concerning marriage recognition was a matter of policy, not judicial remedy. </div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>The government further contended that the refusal did not amount to unconstitutional discrimination, as it was applied equally to all same-sex couples.</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>Colours Caribbean, acting as an interested party, was represented in the appeals by David Cadman, K.C., and Dr Leonardo J. Raznovich. They submitted that the non-recognition of lawfully solemnized same-sex marriages from other jurisdictions was inconsistent with constitutional protections and international human rights norms. </div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>The lawyers urged the court to construe ‘spouse’ inclusively for immigration purposes.</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>Constitutional issues</div>
 <div>The case required the court to consider several constitutional provisions. </div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>"Section 7: Guarantees equality before the law and equal protection and benefit of the law. Section 9: Protects the right to private and family life, subject to certain public interest limitations. Section 16: Prohibits discrimination, including on grounds of sexual orientation, by law or by public officials. Section 10: </div>
 <div>Specifically defines the right to marry as the right of "every unmarried man and woman of marriageable age … to marry a person of the opposite sex”.”</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>The court was also called upon to consider whether it could fashion an effective remedy for infringed rights, including whether it had the power to order legislative amendment.</div>
 <div> </div>
 <div>Implications</div>
 <div>The judgment has significant implications for the legal landscape of the Turks and Caicos Islands and the wider British Overseas Territories. </div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>The ruling establishes that same-sex spouses who are lawfully married abroad are entitled to be recognized as ‘spouses’ for the purpose of residence permits, thereby placing the Islands closer in alignment with international human rights standards and practices in other UK territories.</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>While the judgment does not legalise same-sex marriage in the TCI, it affirms that constitutional protections against discrimination, including based on sexual orientation, must be given full effect by public authorities. </div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>The court’s approach signals growing judicial recognition of LGBTQ+ rights, even in the absence of legislative reform.</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>The court was careful to respect the Constitution's boundaries on the definition of marriage, indicating that substantive changes to marriage law remain a matter for the legislature. </div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
 <div>However, it stressed that administrative decisions, such as the granting of immigration benefits, must comply with higher constitutional norms.</div>
 <div><br />
 </div>
